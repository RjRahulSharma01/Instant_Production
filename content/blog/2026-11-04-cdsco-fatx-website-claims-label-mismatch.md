On 28 October 2026 CDSCO cancelled the import registration of a Korean cosmetic called FatX. The label was not the problem. The importer's website described the product as a treatment that dissolves fat cells and reduces their size, and the regulator ruled that those were drug claims, which a cosmetic cannot make. If your brand page says more than your carton does, this order is about you.

The importer was Tekno Midas Aesthetics of New Delhi, holding registration certificate RC/COS-003300, issued on 15 July 2022 and valid until 14 July 2027. It lost roughly eight months of validity, and with it the legal basis to sell the product in India.

## What happened, in order

The sequence matters more than the headline, because it shows how little time a brand gets once a mismatch is spotted.

CDSCO issued a show-cause notice on 10 August 2026. The importer replied on 1 September. The Central Licensing Authority found that reply "inconclusive and unsatisfactory." The importer then asked to surrender its certificate voluntarily. [ANI's report of the order](https://aninews.in/news/national/general-news/cdsco-cancels-fatxs-registration-over-misleading-claims-on-product20261031093829/) says the authority did not simply accept the surrender and instead cancelled the registration, effective from the date of the order.

That is a difference worth noticing. A voluntary surrender is a clean exit. A cancellation sits on the record.

## The legal chain behind it

Three provisions did the work, and [Business Standard's coverage](https://www.business-standard.com/industry/news/cdsco-cancels-fatx-registration-over-misleading-claims-126110200369_1.html) names all of them.

Section 3(aaa) of the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940 defines a cosmetic by what it is meant to do: cleanse, beautify, promote attractiveness or alter appearance. Rule 36 of the Cosmetics Rules, 2020 says no cosmetic may purport or claim anything that is false or misleading to the intending user. Rule 16 gives the Central Licensing Authority the power to cancel an import registration.

Put together, the logic is short. The website said the product dissolves fat cells. Dissolving fat cells is a physiological claim. A physiological claim makes the product a drug. A drug cannot hold a cosmetic registration. The registration goes.

> A registration describes a product as the regulator approved it. Your website is where you describe the product as you would like it to sell.

## Why the website was the evidence

This is the part most beauty teams have not absorbed. CDSCO did not test the product in this order. As far as the published order and coverage show, it compared two documents: the label approved at registration, and the claims on the importer's own site. They did not match, and the gap was enough.

That has a practical consequence for everyone who writes copy. The label is a frozen document that someone lawyered once. The website is a live document that changes every time a campaign launches, a landing page is tested, or a founder edits a product description at midnight.

Most clients we work with have a compliance check on packaging and none on the page that sells the packaging. The FatX order is what that gap looks like when a regulator reads it.

## This was not a one-off

The same enforcement logic appears in other recent registration cancellations. According to the tracker at [Blacklisted](https://blacklisted.co.in/cosmetics/banned/cosmetics-advertising-claims-rule-36), which links the underlying CDSCO orders, QR 678 Neo was cancelled on 22 December 2025 after being marketed for hair loss after chemotherapy and for androgenetic alopecia. Elementre MD Peel-Tri AHA was cancelled on 11 May 2026 after a 60 per cent acid peel was sold with a patient tolerance claim.

Each case involves claims, not contamination or a bad batch. In each, the product itself may have been unremarkable. The words around it were what crossed the line.

All of this follows CDSCO's public notice of 18 May 2026, covered in [our earlier piece on treatment claims](/blog/cdsco-cosmetic-treatment-claims-2026), which said labels, ads, digital promotions and consumer communication must describe only cosmetic use. FatX is the order where the regulator's own words point at a website as the problem.

## The audit we run on a beauty site

We look at this every time we take over a skincare or haircare account. It takes an afternoon.

| Where the claim lives | What to compare it against |
| --- | --- |
| Product page headline and bullets | Approved label and registration category |
| Meta and Google ad copy | The same page, since ads feed the page |
| Landing page FAQs and "how it works" | Whether any verb implies a body process |
| Creator briefs and approved scripts | The page again, because creators repeat it |
| Marketplace listings | The label, since those are often copied by sellers |

The test on each line is the same. If the sentence describes how skin looks, it is probably cosmetic. If it describes what happens inside the body, such as dissolving, burning, regrowing, repairing or curing, it is a drug claim wearing a cosmetic's registration.

For wording, the safer family of verbs is the one that describes the surface: cleanses, hydrates, smoothes, visibly evens, helps reduce the look of. ## What to do this week

First, export every product page, ad and listing for each registered SKU into one sheet and put the registered label text next to it. Do it per SKU, not per brand.

Second, strike or rewrite anything where the page promises more than the label. Do not wait for a show-cause notice, because the FatX timeline shows you get from 10 August to cancellation in under three months.

Third, assign one person to own the claim library. Not the agency, not the creator, not the founder. One person whose approval is required before any page goes live.

A caveat: the coverage we found does not reproduce the full order text, and we have not seen a published penalty beyond the cancellation. If you hold an import registration and your site is already live with strong claims, read the original order on CDSCO's site and speak to a regulatory consultant before you change anything in bulk.
