The government isn't proposing to ban prescription drug advertising in India. It's proposing to license it. On 16 February 2026, the Drugs Technical Advisory Board's 93rd meeting recommended that Schedule H, H1 and X medicines can only be advertised with the prior sanction of the Central Government, a gate that doesn't currently exist in the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules, 1945.

That's a different problem to the one most coverage described back in November. Early reporting called it an outright ban on online promotion of prescription-only drugs. Read the actual DTAB agenda item and it says something narrower: advertising can continue, but only once a licensed seller, wholesaler or distributor has cleared it with Delhi first.

## What the DTAB actually recommended

Agenda 5 of the 93rd DTAB meeting asked for suitable provisions in the Drugs Rules so that no advertisement of Schedule H, H1 or X drugs happens except with the Central Government's previous sanction. Agenda 14, in the same meeting, dealt with Schedule G on its own terms: rather than finalise a standalone ad-restriction notification for it, the Board agreed to fold Schedule G into Schedule H, so the new sanction rule reaches it automatically once that reclassification lands.

None of this is notified yet. It sits inside CDSCO's own published minutes as a Board recommendation, waiting on the Ministry to draft and gazette the actual rule change. Nothing in the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules changes until that happens, and no timeline for it has been published.

## Why "ban" was the wrong word

Under the existing law, the Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act, 1954 already restricts advertising tied to specific conditions and remedies, and Section 7 sets the penalty: up to six months in prison or a fine for a first conviction, up to a year for a repeat one. That's a conditions-based test. It says nothing about needing government pre-clearance for an entire schedule of drugs regardless of what the ad actually claims.

The DTAB recommendation adds a licensing step on top of that test. A Schedule H ad with no medical claim at all, just a brand name and a chemist locator, would still need sanction under the proposal as drafted. That's a wider net than "no false cure claims," which is why the chemist trade body pushed back within weeks of the minutes becoming public.

The Schedule G to H move is worth a second look on its own. Schedule G already carries a caution label for drugs including certain corticosteroids and antibiotic combinations, and folding it into Schedule H pulls more antibiotic entries into the same 526-molecule prescription-only list right as antimicrobial resistance keeps coming up in the DTAB's own reasoning. A brand naming a specific antibiotic by brand or salt name in ad copy, a product description or an influencer script is exactly the content this reclassification is aimed at, independent of whether a Schedule H sanction rule ever gets gazetted.

## The chemist body's objection

The All India Organisation of Chemists and Druggists filed a consolidated representation against the proposal, reported in early April. Their objection isn't really about false claims. They argue that permitting Schedule H advertising at all, even under sanction, undermines the idea that these are prescription-only medicines in the first place, and that policing digital and indirect advertising at the scale India's e-pharmacy sector now runs at isn't practically enforceable.

> "Encourages reckless self-medication, delays diagnosis and contributes to antimicrobial resistance."

That's the concern SPARSH Hospitals' Dr Ranjan Shetty raised about prescription drug promotion in general, and AIOCD is applying the same logic to the sanction route specifically. A licence to advertise under government approval is still a licence to advertise, and self-medication risk doesn't disappear because Delhi signed off on the campaign.

## What actually changes, by schedule

| Schedule | What it covers | Position today | Under the DTAB recommendation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Schedule G | Caution-label drugs, including certain antibiotics and hormones | A separate ad-restriction notification was drafted, then set aside | Folds into Schedule H, so H's sanction rule applies once reclassified |
| Schedule H | Roughly 526 prescription-only molecules | General DMR Act test applies, no schedule-wide ad ban | No advertisement without the Central Government's prior sanction |
| Schedule H1 | Habit-forming and register-tracked antibiotics | Same general DMR Act test | Same sanction requirement |
| Schedule X | Narcotic and psychotropic substances | Same general DMR Act test, already tightly controlled at the point of sale | Same sanction requirement |

## What this means for a D2C health or telehealth brand right now

Nothing changes today. The Rules haven't been amended, no notification has been gazetted, and [Google's own India healthcare ad policy](/blog/google-ads-online-pharmacy-india-2026) already blocks prescription-drug keywords and drug names in ad copy regardless of what CDSCO eventually decides. If your media plan already avoids naming prescription molecules in creative, this proposal doesn't add work yet.

What's worth doing now: audit any Schedule H, H1 or X adjacent content sitting outside paid media too, landing pages, WhatsApp catalogues, influencer scripts, organic search content that names a molecule or makes a treatment claim without a doctor in the loop. That's the same gap the NMC's own [rules for what a doctor can publish](/blog/nmc-safe-content-for-doctors) close from the other direction. If a sanction requirement lands, it will apply to the seller, wholesaler or distributor of record, not the agency, but the content still has to sit inside the sanctioned version or come down.

Most [D2C health and telehealth clients](/industries/healthcare) we work with already separate OTC-safe content from anything prescription-adjacent for exactly this reason. That separation is what turns a rule change like this into a non-event instead of a scramble.

The honest caveat: DTAB minutes get revised, delayed or quietly dropped more often than they get gazetted on schedule, and AIOCD's opposition is the kind of pushback that has stalled similar proposals before. Track the actual notification in the Gazette, not the meeting minutes, before you change anything client-facing.
