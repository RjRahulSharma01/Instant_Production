From 1 January 2027, a discount on an Indian marketplace has to be shown next to its "prior price": the lowest price that product was actually offered at during the preceding 30 days. Not the MRP. Not the price it launched at two years ago. The Department of Consumer Affairs notified the Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) (Amendment) Rules, 2026 on 10 September 2026, and this is the provision that changes the most work for the most people.

The rules replace the Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020, which were framed under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019. Along with the pricing change, they cover search ranking disclosure, sponsored listing labels, annual dark pattern audits and a set of seller and product disclosures.

## What the prior price rule actually says

When a price reduction is announced, both the reduced price and the prior price have to be displayed. The prior price is defined as the lowest price at which the goods or services were offered in the 30 days before the reduction was announced.

Read that definition carefully, because it does two things at once. It sets a reference point, and it sets a memory. A product that has been sitting at ₹899 for the last three weeks cannot be advertised down from ₹2,499 on 1 January. Its prior price is ₹899, and ₹899 is what the listing has to show.

That is the part most D2C pricing decks have not absorbed yet. The lever was never really the discount percentage. It was the size of the gap between the number you anchored against and the number you charged. This rule caps that gap at whatever you charged last month.

## Why permanent sale pricing stops working

Most catalogues we inherit have one of two pricing shapes. Either the product sits at a high listed price and runs a near permanent 50 to 70 percent "off", or it flips between two prices on a fortnightly rhythm tied to platform sale events.

Both shapes collapse under a 30 day lookback.

| Pricing pattern | What the creative says today | What the listing can claim from 1 Jan 2027 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sits at ₹899, MRP ₹2,499, permanent sale | "64% off" | Prior price ₹899, so no reduction to claim |
| ₹1,499 for 25 days, ₹1,199 for 5 days, repeating | "20% off" during the dip | Prior price ₹1,199 once the cycle has run a month, so the dip is the new normal |
| ₹1,899 steady, genuine cut to ₹1,299 for a festive window | "32% off" | Prior price ₹1,899, claim holds |

Only the third shape survives intact, and it is the least common one in the catalogues we look at. The first two were built to make a discount claim available every single day of the year, which is precisely the behaviour the 30 day window is designed to price out.

> A discount is only a discount relative to a price someone could actually have paid. Everything else was anchoring dressed up as a saving.

## Your ads are downstream of the listing, not exempt from it

Worth being precise about scope here, because it is where a lot of advice is about to get sloppy. These rules bind e-commerce entities and marketplaces. They do not directly regulate your Meta creative or your Google Shopping ad copy.

What they do is make the landing experience tell a different story than the ad. If the ad promises 64% off and the product detail page shows a prior price of ₹899 against a selling price of ₹899, the ad is now a misleading advertisement under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, and the CCPA already has the power to act on that without needing these new rules at all.

We look at this every time we audit a D2C account: the claim in the ad and the claim on the page have to be the same claim. From January the page gets a new source of truth, and the ad has to move to match it.

## The other three provisions that touch marketing

**Sponsored listings need clear and prominent disclosure.** If you are buying marketplace retail media, the label on your placement becomes mandatory rather than platform policy. Expect the label treatment to get larger and more visible, which will move click-through rates on sponsored slots. Budget for that shift rather than reading it as creative fatigue in January.

**Search results cannot be manipulated in a way that misleads users or reduces the relevance of results to the query.** Marketplaces must also publish the main parameters that rank goods and sellers, plus the relative importance of each, in plain and intelligible language. For anyone running marketplace SEO, this is the first time the ranking logic is meant to be readable rather than reverse engineered.

**Annual dark pattern self-audit with a displayed certificate.** Compliance with the Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of Dark Patterns, 2023 stops being advisory. The audit covers false urgency, drip pricing, basket sneaking and the rest of the list, which means the countdown timers and pre-ticked add-ons that sit inside checkout funnels get reviewed on a fixed annual cycle.

There are also disclosure requirements around importer details and full country of origin for imported goods, which we wrote about separately in [the country of origin filter post](/blog/coo-filter-ecommerce-listings-2026), and a bar on bundled fees for services unrelated to the platform, with loyalty and membership programmes carved out.

## What non-compliance has actually cost so far

Section 21 of the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 allows penalties of up to ₹10 lakh for a first violation and up to ₹50 lakh for each subsequent one. The orders issued so far have sat well below those ceilings. The CCPA fined FirstCry ₹2 lakh over discount practices, and Zepto ₹7 lakh for dark patterns in December 2025.

Those numbers are small enough that some platforms have treated them as a cost of doing business. A Datum Intelligence study published in June 2026 estimated that Indian online marketplaces generate ₹25,000 to ₹28,000 crore a year through hidden fees, auto-subscriptions and pre-selected add-ons, and noted that the ₹50 lakh cap is roughly one two-hundredth of what a single dark pattern can generate in a year.

The pressure is not really coming from the fine. It is coming from volume of complaint. The Department of Consumer Affairs reported 17,71,622 grievances to the National Consumer Helpline in 2025, of which 5,11,196, about 29 percent, related to e-commerce. Every e-commerce entity now has to become a partner in the NCH convergence process and hand complainants a copy of their complaint as recorded.

## What we would do between now and January

Pull 90 days of price history for your top 50 SKUs and work out what each one's prior price would be on any given day. That single sheet tells you which products have a real discount to advertise in January and which ones have been running on an anchor.

Then decide, per SKU, whether you want the list price or the discount. You mostly cannot keep both. Products with genuine price elasticity are usually better off at a clean single price with the saving narrative moved to bundles, sizes or subscription, none of which are price reductions and none of which trigger the prior price disclosure.

Finally, rebuild the discount creative library so percentage claims are a variable pulled from the feed rather than baked into the image. Accounts that hard-code "64% OFF" into 300 static assets are going to spend January taking them down. This is worth fixing inside your [performance marketing](/services/performance-marketing) setup now, while there is still a quarter of runway, rather than in the middle of a Republic Day sale.

One honest caveat: the rules were notified two days ago and the industry consultation is not finished. Business Standard reported that executives are already pushing back on the price history requirement, on the ground that individual sellers set and change prices rather than the marketplace. The 30 day definition could be softened or the responsibility could be reallocated. The pricing shapes above are still worth fixing either way, because they were fragile before this rule existed.
