If you courier a creator products worth more than Rs 20,000 in a financial year and they keep them, you owe 10% TDS on the full value — and you owe it *before* the box leaves your warehouse, not after the reel goes up. That obligation used to live in Section 194R of the Income-tax Act, 1961. Since 1 April 2026 it sits in Section 393(1), Table Sl. No. 8(iv) of the Income-tax Act, 2025. The rate and the threshold did not move. The section number, the payment codes and the paperwork did.

Most barter programmes we audit have never deducted a rupee against this. The seeding budget sits inside marketing, the courier goes out from the warehouse, and finance never sees a transaction because no money changed hands.

## What actually triggers the deduction

CBDT Circular No. 12/2022, dated 16 June 2022, drew the line that still governs this. A brand sends a product to a creator so they can use it and make content about it. If the creator **returns** the product after shooting — a review unit, a loaner handset, a dress that goes back — it is not a benefit and there is no TDS. If the creator **keeps** it, it is a benefit or perquisite in their hands, and the brand deducts.

That single word — keeps — is the whole test. It has nothing to do with whether you called it a gift, a sample, a seeding drop, or PR. It has nothing to do with whether the creator posted.

The threshold is Rs 20,000 aggregate, per recipient, per financial year. Cross it and 10% applies to the entire cumulative value, not the excess. A Rs 6,000 Diwali hamper in October and a Rs 16,000 launch kit in February do not sit safely under two separate limits. They add to Rs 22,000, and TDS becomes due on all Rs 22,000.

## The part that breaks the workflow

Ordinary TDS is withheld out of a payment. Barter has no payment. Section 393(1) still requires deduction before the benefit is handed over, which leaves a brand two options, both of which have to happen upstream of the courier.

The first is to collect the tax from the creator in cash before shipping. On a Rs 30,000 kit, that means asking someone who is about to receive "free" product to first transfer you Rs 3,000. Try selling that to a nano creator.

The second is to gross up and bear the tax yourself, which is what almost every brand ends up doing. Return filing has a specific code for exactly this situation — code 1034 covers benefits wholly in kind or where the cash component cannot absorb the deduction. Reporting goes on Form 26Q.

| | Cash creator fee | Barter / product seeding |
|---|---|---|
| Section | 393 (formerly 194J/194C) | 393(1), Sl. No. 8(iv) |
| Rate | 10% (professional fee) | 10% |
| Threshold | Contract-dependent | Rs 20,000 aggregate per creator per FY |
| When | On credit or payment, whichever is earlier | Before the product is handed over |
| Return code | Standard | 1033, or 1034 if wholly in kind |

> The compliance event in a barter campaign happens at the warehouse, not at the invoice. That is why finance never catches it.

## GST is the second bill nobody counts

Income tax is only half of it. A barter is a supply. The creator is providing a service — content, usage rights, a post — and receiving non-monetary consideration. That falls under Rule 27 of the CGST Rules, 2017, which covers value of supply where consideration is not wholly in money: open market value first, and where that is not ascertainable, the value of the goods received.

For the creator this means 18% GST on the deemed value of a deal where they received no cash. It also means the value of every barter counts toward their aggregate turnover for the Rs 20 lakh registration threshold (Rs 10 lakh in special category states). A creator running mostly barter can cross the line without ever having been paid.

We have watched this land badly more than once. A creator accepts twenty PR boxes across a year, hits registration, and discovers a liability against income that arrived as skincare. They then either raise their cash rates to cover it or quietly stop accepting barter. Either way the brand's "free" channel stops being free.

## What to change in the brief

Three things, and none of them require a new tool.

**Decide return-or-retain at brief stage, and write it down.** If the shoot genuinely needs the product only for the shoot — an appliance, a device, a high-value garment — build the return leg into the contract and the reverse courier into the budget. No retention, no benefit, no deduction. This is the cleanest lever available and most brands never pull it, because nobody wants a used unit back. Price that decision honestly: taking the unit back may cost less than the grossed-up tax on it.

**Keep a creator-wise register from the first shipment.** Not a spreadsheet of campaigns — a spreadsheet of recipients, with cumulative retained value and PAN. PAN matters: without it, Section 206AA pushes the rate to 20%. Collect it in the onboarding form, alongside the usage-rights clause you should already be collecting. If you are not, that is a separate and more expensive problem — we wrote about [how missing usage rights waste the whole creator budget](/blog/influencer-content-into-paid).

**Tell the creator the number before they say yes.** A barter offer stated as "Rs 30,000 of product" and a barter offer stated as "Rs 30,000 of product, we bear the TDS, you will need to account for GST on the deemed value" get very different acceptance rates — but the second one does not blow up in month nine. Most creator relationships we have seen sour over compliance, not money, and it is always because someone found out late.

## The honest caveat

Nobody knows how aggressively this will be enforced against seeding programmes specifically. The disallowance risk is the sharper end: under the non-deduction provisions, 30% of the expenditure can be disallowed as a business expense, which for a brand spending Rs 40 lakh a year on product seeding is a considerably worse outcome than the Rs 4 lakh of TDS it was avoiding.

For a [D2C brand](/industries/ecommerce) shipping a few hundred kits a quarter, the practical answer is not to stop seeding. It is to move the compliance step one box earlier — into the brief, where it costs a form field — instead of leaving it for a March scramble.
