The Central Consumer Protection Authority has now treated a missing licence line on a product page as a misleading advertisement. On 16 January 2026 it fined Amazon, Flipkart, Meta (Facebook Marketplace) and Meesho Rs 10 lakh each over walkie-talkie listings that did not state the operating frequency, whether a licence was needed, or whether the device had Equipment Type Approval. On 25 October it fined a Kerala brand, Hogar Technologies and Innovations, Rs 1 lakh for the same omission on its own site.

If you sell anything that needs an approval before it can be sold or used, that second order is the one to read.

## What the CCPA actually found

The Authority opened a suo motu investigation and identified more than 16,900 non-compliant listings across 13 e-commerce entities. Many were personal mobile radios in the 400 to 470 MHz UHF range, which is regulated in India, yet were described as "license-free" or "100% legal."

Only the 446.0 to 446.2 MHz band is exempt from licensing, and the CCPA's order notes that even devices in that band need Equipment Type Approval from the Department of Telecommunications before sale. Anything outside the band cannot be sold without prior approvals.

The listing gap was the whole case. The CCPA did not need to prove anyone was caught using an illegal radio. It was enough that a buyer could not tell, from the page, whether the device was legal to operate.

## The numbers that make this a catalogue problem

The platform figures reported from the January order show how it plays out at scale.

| Entity | What was reported |
|---|---|
| Flipkart | Nearly 66,000 units sold with missing or out-of-band frequency details |
| Amazon | 467 listings without certification details; over 2,600 units sold between January 2023 and May 2025 |
| Meesho | More than 2,200 units sold through a single seller |
| Facebook Marketplace | More than 700 listings delisted after CCPA intervention |

Smaller entities (Chimiya, JioMart, Talk Pro and MaskMan Toys) were fined Rs 1 lakh each. By October the CCPA said final orders had been passed against 12 of the 13 entities it had noticed. Amazon, Flipkart, Meta and TradeIndia have challenged the orders before judicial forums, so the penalty amounts are not settled law yet.

## The defence that did not work

Two arguments were rejected, and both are ones we hear from clients.

The platforms argued they were intermediaries. The CCPA said intermediary protection depends on effective due diligence, and that a platform that lets regulated products be listed, promoted and sold has to run regulatory checks. It directed them to verify frequency and ETA certification before listing, deploy automated monitoring, run self-audits and publish audit certificates.

Hogar argued it held valid regulatory approvals and that its website mainly provided information rather than direct sales. The CCPA's answer, as reported, was that holding approvals does not remove the duty to give complete information before purchase.

> Having the certificate is not the defence. Showing it on the page, before the click, is.

## Why the fine is the smaller number

Rs 1 lakh will not hurt a funded D2C brand. The cost sits in what follows an order: the CCPA published the findings, named the products and platforms, and asked marketplaces to monitor listings automatically. A marketplace under that instruction does not wait for a notice to your brand. It delists SKUs, and a delisted bestseller in the middle of Diwali week costs far more than the penalty ever will. Hogar's was also a brand-owned site, so there was no platform to share the exposure with.

## What this means if you run paid traffic to a product page

Most D2C teams we work with treat the product page as a conversion asset and the compliance file as something that lives with the founder or the CA. This order closes that gap. The page is the advertisement, and the ad that points to it inherits whatever the page leaves out.

The category is wider than radios. The same logic applies to anything where legality of sale or use depends on an approval: BIS-marked electronics, CDSCO-registered cosmetics and devices, FSSAI-licensed foods, drones, trackers, imported gadgets. If the buyer needs to know a licence or certificate exists to use the product lawfully, that fact is material information.

A practical audit takes an afternoon:

1. List every SKU that needs a licence, registration or type approval to be sold or operated.
2. For each, check the page shows the approval number or certificate and states plainly whether the buyer needs a licence.
3. Strip claims like "licence-free" and "100% legal" unless you can attach the specific exemption and its limits to the same sentence.
4. Make sure your ad copy and your page say the same thing. A Meta ad that says "no licence needed" over a page that is silent is two problems, not one.
5. Keep the certificate file where a page editor can reach it, not in a founder's inbox.

## The honest caveat

This was a radio-equipment case, decided on its facts, and the appeals are pending. The CCPA has not said that every product category needs a licence line. What it has said, twice, is that a regulatory approval you hold but do not disclose does not protect the ad.

Do the SKU audit this week, before the festive-season volume sends more traffic to pages that are quiet about the one thing a regulator reads first.
