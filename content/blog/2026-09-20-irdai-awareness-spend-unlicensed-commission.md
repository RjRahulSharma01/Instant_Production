If you pay a partner for "brand awareness" and that partner is not licensed to solicit insurance, IRDAI will read the payment as commission. That is the finding in the Authority's order against Reliance General Insurance Company Limited (Ref. IRDAI/E&C/ORD/MISC/145/12/2025), which carried a penalty of Rs 1 crore. The invoice said advertising. The Authority looked at who received the money and what they were positioned to do with it.

## What the order actually found

The order followed a remote inspection conducted between 27 and 31 December 2021, a show cause notice dated 29 November 2024, and a personal hearing on 5 March 2025 before a panel of two Whole Time Members. Three of the charges turn on the same reasoning.

The insurer paid roughly Rs 5.16 crore during FY 2018-19 to Glitterbug Technologies Pvt Ltd, the parent company of Coverfox Insurance Broking Pvt Ltd, on account of a "Consumer Awareness Programme". The Authority noted the invoiced services had no bearing on the scope of services in the agreement, and that a director of the paying-side entity also held directorship in the broking company.

A second charge covered payments of about Rs 1.14 crore, Rs 27 lakh and Rs 28.67 lakh across FY 2018-19 to FY 2020-21 to an individual who was a licensed agent of a different insurer. The invoices cited customer awareness programmes and event organisation. What sank it was the creative: the agent's name appeared on banners and standees alongside the insurer, which reads to a prospect as if that person sells those policies.

A third charge covered roughly Rs 2.92 crore and Rs 5.33 crore paid to Suvidhaa Infoserve Pvt Ltd, an unlicensed entity, for marketing and advertisement services.

The insurer's defence was consistent across all three: this was branding and awareness, not solicitation, so Section 40(2) of the Insurance Act, 1938 does not apply. The Authority did not accept it.

> The test is not what the deliverable was called. It is whether the person holding the money was in a position to convert attention into a policy.

## Why this lands on the marketing team, not just finance

Most of the spend in question would sit in a media plan. Consumer awareness programmes, event activations, co-branded standees, a partner's placement in a campaign: these are line items a performance lead or a brand manager signs off, often long before compliance sees a rupee of it.

Two things decide the outcome, and marketing controls both. The first is who the counterparty is. The second is whether the creative puts that counterparty's name next to the insurer's in a way that implies distribution. Both are creative and media decisions.

Most clients we work with in regulated categories treat the licence status of a media or activation partner as a procurement question. On insurance briefs it is a creative question too, because the artwork is the evidence.

## The rule the approval chain actually runs on

The current framework sits in the IRDAI (Protection of Policyholders' Interests, Operations and Allied Matters of Insurers) Regulations, 2024, notified on 20 March 2024 and in force from 1 April 2024.

Regulation 27(3) says every distribution channel shall publish advertisements only as soliciting products offered by insurers, and must not give the impression that the products are offered by it directly. Regulation 27(6) extends the same standard to static and interactive content posted on social media pages. Regulation 27(7) makes the insurer responsible for controls and oversight over advertising carried out through its distribution channels.

Regulation 28 is the one that changes timelines. Every advertisement issued by a distribution channel representing the insurer, or by an insurance intermediary, requires the insurer's written approval before it goes out.

Under the May 2023 amendment to the 2021 advertisement regulations, that approval runs through an advertisement committee with at least three members drawn from marketing, actuarial and compliance, reporting into the Product Management Committee. The PMC is the final authority to approve or reject. The insurer maintains a register of advertisements filed, approved, rejected and withdrawn, keeps records for at least three years from the date of withdrawal, and uploads approved advertisements to its website in chronological URN order within three days of release.

| Decision | Who signs it in a normal brand | Who signs it under the 2024 regulations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Product claim in a reel | Brand lead | Advertisement committee, then PMC |
| Aggregator or broker running their own creative | The partner | Insurer, in writing, before release |
| Partner logo lockup on a standee | Design team | Insurer, as a solicitation risk |
| Paying an activation partner | Procurement | Section 40(2) exposure if unlicensed |

Two practical consequences. A three-day upload obligation means the live creative and the approved creative have to be the same file, so late caption edits on a reel are a compliance event. A three-year retention obligation means the asset trail matters as much as the asset.

## Enforcement is now a faster process

On 10 September 2026, IRDAI issued a notification (F. No IRDAI/DoP/13/227/2026) under Section 23(1) of the IRDA Act, 1999, delegating specified penalty and direction powers. Directions to an insurer or insurance intermediary under Section 34 in an enforcement proceeding for identified violations now sit with the Chairperson or a Whole Time Member. Penalties under Section 102, Section 104 for contraventions of Sections 27 and 27E, and decisions on quantum under Sections 105C(2) and 105D go to a panel of the Chairperson and one Whole Time Member, or two Whole Time Members.

That is an internal routing change, not a new advertising rule. It matters anyway. The Reliance General order ran from inspection in December 2021 to order in December 2025. Delegation exists to shorten exactly that kind of gap, which means the distance between a campaign going live and a consequence arriving is narrowing.

## What to change before the next insurance brief ships

Ask for the licence status of every entity that receives campaign money, in writing, before the media plan is approved. Agent, broker, corporate agent, web aggregator, unlicensed activation vendor: each of those has a different answer and only one of them is a free choice.

Look at the artwork for co-branding. If a partner's name, photograph or contact number appears next to the insurer's on anything a prospect sees, assume a regulator will read it as solicitation, whatever the agreement says.

Build the approval lead time into the calendar rather than treating it as a formality at the end. An advertisement committee plus a PMC sign-off is not a same-day turnaround, and the three-day upload window starts from release, not from approval.

The honest caveat: an order against one insurer on facts from FY 2018-19 to FY 2020-21 is not a rule, and the Authority reasons case by case. But the reasoning in that order is not exotic. It applies the plain text of Section 40(2) to a set of invoices, and the 2024 regulations have since made insurer oversight of distribution-channel advertising explicit. If your campaign structure only works when nobody reads it that way, it is not a campaign structure.

We look at this every time a [fintech](/industries/fintech) or insurance client briefs a partner-led activation, and the [performance marketing](/services/performance-marketing) plan is usually where the exposure is created.
