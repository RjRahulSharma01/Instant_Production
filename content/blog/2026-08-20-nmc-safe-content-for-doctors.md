A registered medical practitioner in India can publish educational content freely. What they cannot do is solicit patients, claim treatment outcomes, assert superiority over another practitioner, use scare tactics, or show before-and-after images without written consent obtained after the fact. That distinction — educate, do not solicit — is the whole regulation in one line, and almost every clinic that gets into trouble has crossed it in a way that was obvious in hindsight.

The confusion is worth clearing up because it costs practices real money. Most doctors we speak to believe one of two wrong things: either that marketing is banned outright, or that everyone else is doing it so it must be fine. Both lead to bad decisions. The first leaves a good practice invisible. The second leaves a good practice exposed to a complaint at the state medical council.

## Where the rule actually comes from

The operative document is the Indian Medical Council (Professional Conduct, Etiquette and Ethics) Regulations, 2002, chapter 6, now administered by the National Medical Commission. The [complete compliance guide from Branding Pioneers](https://brandingpioneers.com/blog/mci-nmc-advertising-marketing-guidelines-for-doctors-india-complete-guide) walks the clauses in detail, and the short version is that clause 6.1 governs advertising and soliciting.

A breach is not a fine in the usual sense. It is a disciplinary matter, and the state medical council can act on it up to and including suspension or removal from the register. That is a different order of risk from a marketing mistake in any other industry. An e-commerce brand that overclaims gets an ASCI notice. A doctor who overclaims can lose the ability to practise.

The Advertising Standards Council of India sits on top of this for the advertising itself. Between the two, the prohibited list is consistent: no superiority claims, no promises of successful treatment, no scare tactics, no patient before-and-afters without documented consent.

## What is explicitly allowed

Educational content. That carve-out is not a loophole, it is the stated position — content explaining a condition, a procedure, what recovery looks like, what a patient should ask before consenting to something, is permitted.

In practice that is most of what a patient actually searches for. Nobody types "best cardiologist who guarantees results". They type "is angioplasty or bypass better for my father", "how long does knee replacement recovery take", "what does a high creatinine reading mean". Those are educational questions and a doctor is the most credible possible answer to them.

The regulation is not really constraining demand generation. It is constraining a specific and fairly narrow set of claims that were never very persuasive anyway.

## The line, drawn in actual sentences

The abstraction is where people go wrong, so here it is concretely.

| Do not publish | Publish instead |
| --- | --- |
| "Best knee replacement surgeon in Pune" | "How knee replacement decisions are made, and when to wait" |
| "98% success rate in IVF" | "What IVF success rates mean, and why clinic numbers are not comparable" |
| "Book now — limited slots this month" | "What to bring to a first consultation" |
| Patient before-and-after, no consent on file | The same images, with written consent obtained after selection, and stated |
| "Do not ignore these symptoms or it may be fatal" | "These symptoms are worth a consultation, and here is why" |

The right-hand column is not weaker content. It is better content. The left-hand column reads like every other clinic and converts badly, because a patient choosing a surgeon is not responding to urgency copy — they are trying to establish whether this person knows what they are doing.

> The compliant version of a healthcare ad is usually the one that would have performed better anyway. Restrictions on claims mostly remove things that were not working.

## Institution-level, not individual-level

One practical structure matters more than any copywriting rule. NMC restrictions bite hardest on individual practitioners soliciting patients. They are considerably looser on institutions promoting their specialities and services.

So a hospital or clinic can run paid campaigns for a department. An individual doctor running paid ads for themselves is on much thinner ice. [Several practitioner guides](https://ichelonconsulting.com/insights/healthcare-social-media-marketing-india-2026) now recommend structuring every campaign at the institution level for exactly this reason, and it is the first thing we change when we pick up a healthcare account.

This has a knock-on effect on landing pages. If the ad is for the department, the page should be about the department and the condition, not a doctor's biography with a booking button. Most clinics have this backwards.

## Meta's own layer, which is separate

Healthcare advertising is fully permitted on Meta in India. What changed over the last two years is how. Health-categorised advertisers face content restrictions on claims and imagery, and — the part that actually hurts — limits on conversion tracking and optimisation.

That last one is worth sitting with. If you cannot optimise for a booking event the way a D2C brand optimises for a purchase, your paid campaigns are working with a duller instrument. The practical answer is to feed the algorithm something it can still see: a longer, more educational content journey, retargeting from content readers rather than cold prospecting, and a higher tolerance for lead quality variance in the first month. The clinics that fight this by trying to force direct-response tactics through a restricted account tend to burn budget for a quarter and then blame the platform.

## The channel nobody is fighting over

While clinics argue about whether they can run ads, the highest-intent demand in healthcare sits somewhere almost entirely unregulated and largely uncontested: local search.

A patient looking for a paediatrician does not begin on Instagram. They search, and they look at the map results, and they read reviews written by people in their own neighbourhood. None of that is advertising in the regulatory sense. Keeping accurate opening hours, correct department listings, real photographs of the actual premises, and responding to reviews without discussing any individual's clinical details is entirely permitted.

Most hospitals we look at have a Google Business Profile that was set up once in 2019 by someone who has left, listing a phone number that rings in a department that no longer exists. Meanwhile the marketing budget goes to display.

The review question comes up constantly, so to be clear about it: asking a satisfied patient to leave a review is not the same as publishing a testimonial claiming an outcome. Do not offer anything in exchange, do not draft the words, do not respond to a negative review by disclosing anything about that patient's treatment. Within those limits, actively maintaining local presence is the single cheapest source of qualified consultations available to an Indian clinic, and the compliance surface is close to zero.

The same logic applies to the doctor's own written explanations of conditions they treat. That content ranks, it is quotable by an AI assistant answering a patient's question, and it is squarely inside the educational carve-out.

## What to actually do this quarter

Start with an audit rather than a campaign. Pull every live page, ad and Instagram post and sort each one into: educational, borderline, or soliciting. Most practices find something in the third bucket that has been sitting there for two years because a previous agency wrote it and nobody reread it.

Then build the content around the questions patients type, not the procedures you want to sell. A useful test: if the piece would still be worth publishing if you were not accepting new patients, it is educational. If it only makes sense as a customer acquisition asset, look at it again.

Get consent documentation into the process now, not later. Written, obtained after the treatment, held on file, and referenced when the image is used. This takes ten minutes per case and removes the single most common exposure.

And be honest with your agency about which side of the line they are asked to work on. An agency that promises you aggressive healthcare performance marketing in India either does not know the regulation or does not care which of you carries the risk. It is not them who gets called before the council.
