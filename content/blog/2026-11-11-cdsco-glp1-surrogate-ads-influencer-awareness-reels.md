If your brief for a weight-loss clinic, a telehealth app or a pharma client includes "obesity awareness" reels, a creator talking about her injection journey, or a celebrity telling people to ask their doctor, the regulator has already told you what it thinks of that. CDSCO's advisory of March 2026 on GLP-1 receptor agonists treats disease-awareness, influencer and corporate campaigns as promotion when they build recall for a prescription drug. The creative being unbranded does not save it.

Eight months on, generic semaglutide is on pharmacy shelves, prices have fallen, and the demand for "weight-loss content" in our inbox has gone up rather than down. So this is worth setting out plainly.

## What the advisory actually says

The Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation issued the advisory on 11 March 2026, nine days before semaglutide went off patent in India on 20 March. It is addressed to manufacturers, importers and marketing authorisation holders of GLP-1 drugs, which covers the Mounjaro, Wegovy and Ozempic brands and the dozens of semaglutide generics that followed.

It bars direct and indirect promotion of these prescription-only medicines to the public across print, electronic, digital and social media. Crucially, it names the formats that had been doing the indirect work: disease-awareness campaigns, influencer engagement and corporate campaigns that create brand recall or product visibility. Per Moneylife's reading of the text, five things may attract action:

1. Promoting prescription-only medicines to the general public.
2. Exaggerating therapeutic efficacy.
3. Suggesting weight loss is assured or guaranteed.
4. Downplaying diet, exercise and behavioural change.
5. Inducing demand for drug therapy.

The legal hooks are the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940 and the Drugs Rules, 1945. By 24 March, News On AIR reported that the Drugs Controller had started targeted actions with state regulators across retail pharmacies, online platforms, wholesalers and wellness clinics, with licence cancellation, penalties and prosecution on the table.

## Why "we never named the drug" is a weaker defence than it looks

Agencies have leaned for years on a reasonable reading of the Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act, 1954. In Vanitha Malayalam Fortnightly v. State of Kerala, the Kerala High Court held there is no DMR Act offence where an advertisement names no drug. That is the logic behind every unbranded "talk to your doctor about obesity" spot.

The March advisory sidesteps that argument. It is not written under the DMR Act at all. It works through the drug licensing regime, and it targets campaigns by their effect: does the activity create recall or visibility for a product that only a registered medical practitioner can prescribe? When two companies hold most of the market for a molecule, an obesity campaign in their colour palette, launched the month generics arrive, does exactly that.

> An unbranded campaign is not unbranded if the only product the viewer can go and ask for is yours.

The exposure is not trivial either. Section 27 of the Drugs and Cosmetics Act carries imprisonment of one to two years and a fine of at least Rs 20,000 for the relevant offences, and Section 31 allows confiscation of stock, according to a Cyril Amarchand Mangaldas analysis of the framework. Those provisions are aimed at the licence holder, but the clinic, the app and the agency are the ones who wrote and placed the content.

## Where creators and clinics sit

The advisory is addressed to drug companies, yet its practical bite lands on everyone in their marketing chain. Fittr's CEO Jitendra Chouksey told Business Standard in March that companies, influencers and doctors were still running GLP-1 ads after the ban without consequences. That is the gap state drug controllers were asked to close, and the content they find first is the content that is public: reels, clinic landing pages and search ads.

Three patterns we see in briefs carry most of the risk.

### The transformation reel

A creator posts her before and after, credits "my weekly shot", and tags a clinic. Even with an ad label and a disclaimer, this hits at least three of the five triggers: it promotes a prescription drug to lay viewers, it implies the result is assured, and it says nothing about diet or monitoring. Delhi bariatric surgeon Dr Atul Peters described exactly this genre to Business Standard as swaying viewers while not technically being an ad. Under the advisory, paid or gifted, it is.

### The clinic or telehealth funnel

"Doctor-led weight loss, GLP-1 programmes from Rs X a month" is a prescription drug offer dressed as a consultation. A telehealth app that runs a lead form promising a prescription after a ten-minute call is inducing demand for drug therapy in the plainest sense. The News On AIR report notes the drug is approved on prescription from endocrinologists and internal medicine specialists, with cardiologists for some indications. A funnel built to route around that is the thing regulators are inspecting.

### The doctor-creator

A physician explaining how GLP-1 drugs work is not automatically a problem. A physician whose explainer ends in "book with me" and a price card is, and it also runs into the NMC's professional conduct rules for practitioners, which we have written about separately.

## What still works

None of this means a weight-management client cannot advertise. It means the drug cannot be the hero, explicitly or by implication. Here is how we now split briefs.

| Content type | Our call | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Creator reel crediting an injection for weight loss | Do not run | Public promotion of a prescription drug, implies an assured result |
| "Ask your doctor about obesity" spot funded by a GLP-1 maker | Do not run | Named in the advisory as surrogate promotion |
| Clinic ad naming a molecule, brand or price per dose | Do not run | Promotes a prescription drug and induces demand |
| Clinic ad for a multidisciplinary programme (dietitian, physician, coaching), no drug named | Run, with care | Service advertising, provided outcomes are not promised |
| Specialist explaining risks, side effects and who should not take these drugs | Run, with review | Education that does not induce demand, if no booking push is attached |
| CME, conferences and detailing to registered practitioners | Permitted channel | Promotion to RMPs is the route the law allows |

Business Standard reported in March that companies were already pivoting to doctor conferences, CME programmes and patient support drives. That is the legitimate home for product messaging. Consumer-facing creative has to be about the service, the care team and the process, never the injection.

Two wording rules carry most of the weight. First, no outcomes: drop "lose 10 kg", "guaranteed", "in weeks" and any before and after imagery. ASCI's annual complaints report for 2025-26 already flags extreme weight-loss claims backed by AI-generated before and after visuals in the food and beverage category; the same imagery attached to a drug is worse. Second, lifestyle has to be load-bearing, not a footnote. If diet, activity and medical monitoring are not part of the main message, you are downplaying them, which is trigger four.

## The brief we now send to creators

For any weight-management campaign through our [influencer marketing](/services/influencer-marketing) desk, the creator brief carries four non-negotiables:

- No drug names, molecule names, brand names, pens, vials or packaging on camera, including in the background.
- No personal before and after, no kilogram numbers and no timeframes.
- The ad label goes on the post, and for health content the creator's qualifications line applies under ASCI's guidelines, which we set out in [our piece on credentials for health and finance creators](/blog/asci-health-finance-creator-credentials-line).
- The content routes to a consultation with the clinic's medical team, never to a "programme" priced around a drug.

We also ask the client, in writing, whether any part of the budget comes from a drug manufacturer or importer. If it does, the campaign is surrogate promotion by definition, and we decline it.

## One honest caveat

The advisory is an administrative direction, and its exact reach over clinics and creators who are not licence holders has not yet been tested in court. A pending Supreme Court challenge to the DMR Act (Nitin Upadhyay v. Union of India) could also reshape the wider medical advertising regime. But waiting for case law is a poor strategy when the downside sits on your client's drug licence and the upside is one reel.
