If your pack, website or ad says "100%" anything, you now need a document that proves it, and the document has to cover the whole product, not one ingredient. Two regulators made that clear within seven weeks of each other. The Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA) fined two food brands in June 2026, and in August the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) barred Dabur from selling products that carried the same kind of claim.

## What the CCPA actually decided

On June 18, 2026, the CCPA passed orders against Storia Foods and Beverages and Mrs. Bectors Food Specialities, the maker of English Oven bread. Each was fined ₹1 lakh and told to withdraw the claims from packaging, websites and digital platforms immediately.

Storia's "100% Tender Coconut Water" was made from 9.6% coconut water concentrate, reconstituted with water. The word "reconstituted" appeared, but only in fine print. English Oven's "100% Atta Bread" and "100% Whole Wheat Bread" contained 87% whole wheat flour, which the company admitted.

The CCPA applied the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, treating the claims as a misleading advertisement under Section 2(28) and an unfair trade practice under Section 2(47). It also relied on its 2022 Guidelines for Prevention of Misleading Advertisements and Endorsements. The order against Storia additionally cited the consumer's right to information under Section 2(9), and noted that health claims on its labels had no product-specific scientific substantiation.

## The four findings that matter for copywriters

Law firm commentary on the orders (we read the S.S. Rana and Chambers write-ups) pulls out the reasoning, and it is more useful than the penalty.

First, "100%" is treated as an absolute number. There is no room for rounding, approximation or "it's a marketing phrase."

Second, the test is how an ordinary consumer reads it, not what the brand meant. Good faith is not a defence.

Third, the fine print cannot rescue a headline. Storia's "reconstituted" disclosure did not cure a front-of-pack claim that said the opposite.

Fourth, the CCPA rejected the "sole grain source" argument from Mrs. Bectors. The company said 100% referred to the grain used, with no other grain in the bread. The CCPA said a shopper reads "100% Whole Wheat" as describing the whole loaf, and "Zero Maida" next to it reinforced that reading. Claims are judged together, for their combined effect.

> A qualifier that only a lawyer would find does not change what a shopper walked away believing.

## Then FSSAI went after a bigger name

On August 3 and 4, 2026, FSSAI issued a prohibition order against Dabur India. It told the company to stop selling the products named in its notice, and any other food product carrying misleading "100%" claims, and to file an Action Taken Report within 15 days.

The products named included honey, apple cider vinegar, virgin coconut oil, sesame oil, cow ghee, coconut water and coconut milk. FSSAI cited the FSS (Advertising and Claims) Regulations, 2018, and said "100 per cent" claims are ambiguous, unverifiable and likely to mislead.

Two details stand out. Website copy such as "100 per cent Natural," "100 per cent Pure" and "100 per cent Organic" was named, so this is not only about packaging. And FSSAI said it had already sent a notice and did not see satisfactory corrective action, which is why it escalated. Dabur said it was checking the content against its website.

The order also flagged organic honey and apple cider vinegar for showing the Jaivik Bharat logo without valid organic endorsement, and called "100 per cent Purity" on coconut milk impermissible because it is a compound food.

## Why a ₹1 lakh fine is the wrong number to look at

The fine is small. Under the Act the CCPA can penalise a misleading advertiser up to ₹10 lakh for a first offence, and it chose a low figure. The expensive part is the withdrawal direction. Packaging runs, e-commerce listings, A+ content, website copy, influencer scripts and paid creatives all have to change, usually in days.

That is where agencies and in-house teams feel it. A claim that went onto a hero image in March ends up on forty marketplace SKUs, three landing pages and a library of creator briefs by October.

## A claims audit you can run this week

Pull every instance of "100%", "pure", "natural", "zero", "no added" and "only" from your live assets. That means pack artwork, PDPs, ad libraries, landing pages, and the briefs you sent to creators. Then do three things with each one.

1. **Find the proof.** For every absolute claim, there should be a lab report, a formulation sheet or a supplier declaration on file, and it should cover the finished product. If the answer is "the main ingredient is 100% X," that is not the same claim.
2. **Read it as a shopper.** Cover the fine print and ask what the front of the pack promises. If the fine print contradicts it, change the front.
3. **Check the claim against the product type.** FSSAI called "100 per cent Purity" impermissible on a compound food. Single-ingredient and multi-ingredient products need different language.

Rewrite what fails with a statement you can prove: the actual percentage ("made with 87% whole wheat flour"), the specific attribute ("no added sugar" where that is true and permitted), or the sourcing fact ("single-origin forest honey" with the paperwork behind it).

Most clients we work with find the problem is rarely the pack. It is the 14 places the claim was copied to.
