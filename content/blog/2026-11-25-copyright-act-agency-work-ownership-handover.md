Paying an agency does not, by itself, make you the owner of what it made. Under Section 17 of India's Copyright Act, 1957, the author is the first owner of a work, and only some commissioned work (films and photographs among them) passes to the client by default. Scripts, graphics, music and copy need a written assignment. We hand all of it to our clients, with masters, versioned exports and prompt documentation.

## What the Act actually says

Section 17 opens with a simple rule: "the author of a work shall be the first owner of the copyright therein." Three provisos then carve out exceptions.

Proviso (b) is the one most brands rely on without knowing it. Where a photograph, a painting or portrait, an engraving or a cinematograph film is made "for valuable consideration at the instance of" a person, that person is the first owner, "in the absence of any agreement to the contrary". So if you commission a brand film and pay for it, the film itself is likely yours unless the contract says otherwise.

Proviso (c) covers employees. Work made in the course of employment under a contract of service belongs to the employer. An agency is not your employee, so this one protects the agency, not you: the agency owns what its own staff make.

Everything else falls back to the main rule. The script, the storyboard, the logo animation, the illustration in your carousel, the copy on your landing page and the original music track are literary, artistic or musical works. Unless they are assigned to you in writing, the person or company that created them owns them.

## Why the paperwork matters more than the invoice

Sections 18 and 19 set out how ownership moves. An owner "may assign to any person the copyright either wholly or partially", but under Section 19(1) no assignment is valid "unless it is in writing signed by the assignor". Section 19(2) says the assignment must specify the rights assigned and their duration and territorial extent.

Then come the defaults that catch brands out:

| If the assignment does not say... | The Act presumes |
|---|---|
| How long it lasts | Five years from the date of assignment, Section 19(5) |
| Where it applies | India only, Section 19(6) |
| Anything about unused rights | Rights not exercised within one year are deemed to lapse, Section 19(4) |

Read those together. A loose clause saying "all creative belongs to the client" with no duration can run out after five years. A cut you never used inside a year can revert. A campaign you later run for customers in the Gulf may sit outside an assignment that only presumes India.

> An invoice proves you paid. Only a signed assignment proves you own it.

This is a summary of the statute, not legal advice. Have your own lawyer read any agency contract before you sign it.

## The ownership gaps we see most often

Most disputes are not dramatic. They show up when a brand changes agency and asks for its files.

**The edit but not the project.** The brand receives the final MP4s. The project files, layered design files and raw footage stay on the agency's drive. The next team has to rebuild every variant from scratch.

**Freelancer chains.** The agency used a freelance motion designer or composer. The agency never took an assignment from them, so it cannot assign what it does not own.

**Creator content with no usage terms.** A creator's reel performs well, the brand wants to run it as a paid ad, and nobody agreed whitelisting, editing rights or a renewal price before the brief.

**Assets held in the agency's business account.** On Meta, the business that owns an ad account or Page is the one that controls it. Meta's developer documentation states plainly: "You cannot grant access to assets of which you are just an `AGENCY`." If the agency created the ad account inside its own business portfolio, your campaign history and audiences live under its control.

**AI work with no record of how it was made.** An agency produces AI visuals or AI video and hands over only the outputs. Without the prompts, model settings and reference inputs, nobody can produce a consistent next batch, and you have nothing to show if anyone asks how an asset was created.

## How we handle ownership at Instant Production

Clients own everything we make for them. That is a standing working principle, not a clause we negotiate.

In practice the handover includes:

1. **Masters.** Full-resolution finals of every film, edit and design, not only the compressed exports used on platforms.
2. **Versioned exports.** Each variant labelled by hook, format and aspect ratio, so a vertical cut and its horizontal twin can be matched months later. This matters because we plan creative volume for fatigue, and a hook bank is only useful if you can find the hooks.
3. **Prompt documentation.** For AI content strategy, AI video and AI generated ads, we document the prompts and inputs behind the assets we deliver. That is what lets an in-house team, or a future agency, continue the work without starting over.
4. **Rights agreed before a creator is briefed.** For influencer work, usage duration, whitelisting, editing rights and renewal pricing are settled before the brief goes out. That is how the best creator content can move into paid without a second negotiation.

Two more points follow from the same principle. Strategy and websites need no retainer, so a client can take a content strategy or a finished site and run it themselves. And where we work alongside an in-house team, which is common, we run creative volume and testing while the in-house team owns the account and the budget.

We take this position because retention should come from the work. Our published client retention is 98%, and it would mean very little if clients stayed because leaving was expensive.

## What buyers say, and why ownership belongs in the brief

The Setup 2025 Marketing Relationship Survey, run with the CMO Institute, found that 61% of respondents had ended an agency relationship over delivery and 61% over value, up from 36% in 2024. The same survey found 96% consider more than one agency when they choose. If most relationships end, the exit terms are part of the deal you are choosing, not a detail for later.

The 6sense 2025 Buyer Experience Report adds that buying groups rank preferred vendors before first contact in 94% of cases. That means the questions below are worth asking in the first conversation, while you are still comparing.

## Seven questions to ask any agency before you sign

1. Will you assign copyright in all deliverables to us in writing, for the full term of copyright, worldwide?
2. Does that include scripts, storyboards, design files, music and copy, not only finished films?
3. Do you hold written assignments from every freelancer and subcontractor who works on our account?
4. Will we receive masters, project files and versioned exports, and in what format?
5. For AI-made assets, will you hand over the prompts and inputs used?
6. Which business owns the ad accounts, Pages and datasets, and can you work as a partner inside ours?
7. For creator content, are usage duration, whitelisting, editing rights and renewal pricing agreed before the brief?

If an agency hesitates on any of these, that tells you something about how the exit will go.

## Where to start

Pull out your current agency contract and look for one sentence: the copyright assignment. Check whether it is signed, whether it names a duration and a territory, and whether it covers more than the final videos. If any of those is missing, ask for a written assignment now, while the relationship is good.
