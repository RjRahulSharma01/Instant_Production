Festive connected TV spend in India is estimated at about Rs 1,200 crore this year, up from Rs 180 crore in 2022. The buying window is four to six weeks. If your CTV plan is measured on impressions delivered, you are paying premium rates for a number that fraud can inflate without anyone noticing until November.

This is not an argument against CTV. It is an argument for changing what you ask the seller to prove before Diwali money moves.

## The numbers behind the worry

The figures come from two places. The first is DoubleVerify's 2026 Global Insights report, "Must-CTV: Streaming's Shift From Promise to Performance", released on 7 May 2026. It found 140% more CTV fraud schemes and variants in Q1 2026 than in Q1 2025, and roughly ten times as many fraudulent CTV apps in 2025 as in 2024.

The second is a BestMediaInfo piece from 16 September 2026 on India's festive CTV boom, which pulls in DoubleVerify's regional data and a campaign analysis from the verification firm mFilterIt.

The detail that matters for an Indian buyer is the fraud type. In DoubleVerify's data, data-centre traffic accounted for 98% of violations in APAC. In North America, bots made up 82%. Verification set up for a US-style bot problem will not necessarily catch what shows up here.

## Direct deals are not a safe harbour

Many planners assume a direct or private-marketplace deal is cleaner than open auction. DoubleVerify's report says otherwise. In three 2025 direct deals it audited, bots accounted for 34% of impressions in a consumer healthcare campaign, 25% in an FMCG campaign and 14% in a pharma campaign.

Those are individual campaigns, not market averages, and the report does not claim otherwise. But they are enough to retire the idea that a negotiated rate card means verified delivery.

The same report found 9% of CTV video impressions in Q4 2025 were served in "TV-off" states, meaning a device kept requesting ads after the screen went dark. Protected campaigns showed a fraud and sophisticated invalid traffic rate under 1% in 2025, against 9% for unprotected ones. Viewability was 94% against 81%.

> A premium inventory cannot be evaluated only by whether an ad call was successfully made or if an impression was counted. Amit Relan, CEO and co-founder of mFilterIt

## Fraud is one leak, frequency is another

The mFilterIt analysis in the BestMediaInfo piece covered 6.02 million ad requests from one campaign. It found 15.86% of impressions exceeded the frequency cap, and 16.47% of delivery was prevented once frequency and invalid-traffic filters were combined. One smart TV generated more than 7,600 ad requests in a single day.

Data-centre and VPN activity was only 0.61% of traffic in that campaign. So the larger leak was not bots at all. It was the same household seeing the same spot far too often.

The article says plainly that these figures come from a single campaign and do not describe Indian CTV as a whole. We read it the same way. It shows what to test for, not what the average is.

| Leak | What it looks like | What to ask for |
|---|---|---|
| Invalid traffic | Data-centre or bot requests counted as impressions | Pre-bid and post-bid IVT blocking, reported by app |
| TV-off delivery | Ad requests after the screen is off | Viewability and "screen on" signal in reporting |
| Frequency overrun | One device or household hit far beyond the cap | Cross-publisher frequency cap, device-level logs |
| App opacity | You cannot see which app served the ad | App-level transparency in the delivery report |

## What we would put in the CTV brief this season

Most clients we work with write CTV briefs the way they write linear TV briefs: a reach target, a CPM and a flight. For the next six weeks we would add four lines.

First, invalid traffic as an explicit KPI. DoubleVerify's survey found only 21% of advertisers treat IVT or fraud detection as a CTV KPI. If it is not in the brief, nobody reports on it.

Second, independent verification on every deal, direct ones included. Name the vendor in the insertion order and ask for the report to be shared with you, not summarised by the seller.

Third, a frequency cap that works across publishers, not just inside each one. The 15.86% overrun above is what happens when each seller counts only its own impressions.

Fourth, a make-good clause tied to the verified number. Without one, the report is just a document.

## Judge it by what happens after the ad

For D2C and quick-commerce brands, the check that matters most is downstream. Run a geo or household holdout on a slice of the CTV budget and compare branded search and direct traffic against it. If delivery looks strong and the lift is flat, the impressions are not the problem you think they are, or they are not real.
