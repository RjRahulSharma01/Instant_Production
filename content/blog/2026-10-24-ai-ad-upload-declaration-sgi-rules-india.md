Since 20 February 2026, any platform in India with more than five million registered users has to ask uploaders whether a video is AI-generated, check the answer with its own tools, and label what turns out to be synthetic. For ad teams, that moves the labelling decision from the edit suite to the upload screen, and it is no longer only your call.

This is separate from ASCI's advertising code. It comes from the IT Rules, and it applies to the pipe your ad travels through.

## What the February amendment actually says

MeitY notified the amendment to the IT (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021 on 10 February 2026, and it came into force on 20 February. It introduced a new category called "synthetically generated information" (SGI): AI-created or AI-altered audio, visual or audio-visual content that appears real. Deepfakes and cloned voices are the headline cases.

Text is out of scope. So is routine editing. Colour correction, noise reduction, compression, formatting and transcription are excluded as long as they do not materially change the substance. Accessibility work such as translation or clearer audio is excluded on the same condition.

The October 2025 draft had proposed a label covering 10% of the frame. The final rules dropped that number. Non-prohibited SGI must instead be "clearly and prominently labelled", with a visual label for visual content and an audio disclosure for audio.

## The part that changes your workflow

Significant social media intermediaries (SSMIs) are platforms with over five million registered users in India. Three duties apply to them.

First, they must require every uploader to declare whether the content is SGI. Second, they must run technical measures, including automated tools, to verify that declaration, and cannot rely on it alone. Third, they must label content that is confirmed as synthetic.

The earlier "endeavour" language is gone. It is now a mandatory duty to implement reasonable and appropriate technical measures, and knowingly letting violating content through counts as a due diligence failure for the platform.

Most clients we work with assumed this was a creator problem. It is also an advertiser problem, because a platform that has to verify declarations will treat a mismatch as a risk to itself and act on it.

## Where ad teams get caught

| Situation | Likely treatment | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Fully AI-generated spokesperson or voice that looks real | SGI. Declare and label | Tick the declaration, keep the on-screen label |
| Real footage, AI background swap that looks real | SGI if it appears real | Declare it |
| Real footage, colour grade and noise reduction | Excluded routine editing | No declaration needed, keep a note of the tools used |
| Real footage, AI translation or dubbing | Excluded only if substance is unchanged and no material part is altered | Check the meaning and the voice match before you skip the declaration |
| Text-only AI copy | Out of scope for SGI | No SGI label |

The grey zone is cloned voice. A Hindi dub in the founder's own cloned voice is accessibility work if the words are faithful, but it is also a voice that was never recorded. We treat that as a declare-it case.

## Metadata is now part of the rule

The amendment also asks for permanent metadata or unique identifiers embedded in SGI where technically feasible, linking it to the tool that generated it. Platforms are not allowed to enable removal or modification of those markers.

That has a practical consequence for agencies. If your workflow exports from a generator, re-renders in an editor and then compresses for upload, check what survives. Provenance data can disappear at the re-render step, and most editors do not warn you when it does. If the file arrives at the platform with no marker and an uploader declaration of "not AI", the platform's detection tools are the only thing left, and you do not control their verdict.

## Who carries the risk

The duty sits with the platform, but the consequence lands on the advertiser. A video that is mislabelled, held for review or taken down in the middle of a sale week costs you reach you have already paid for. That is why the declaration deserves the same sign-off step as legal review, with a named person ticking the box rather than whoever happens to be uploading at midnight.

## The October advisory widens the net

On 8 October 2026 MeitY issued an advisory (File No. 16(2)/2026-CyberLaws) asking social media intermediaries to identify and label or contextualise manipulated, synthetically generated or misleading content, especially when it is presented as depicting a current event. It takes effect immediately.

Unlike the February rules, the advisory's labelling clause does not carry the "appears real" limit, according to MediaNama's reading. It also names no penalty. Treat it as a signal of how aggressively platforms may moderate during sensitive news cycles, not as a new compliance category for ads. If your festive creative uses AI-generated crowds, locations or news-style footage, that is where a label can be applied to you without your input.

## What we would do before the next flight goes live

Keep a one-page production record per AI ad: which tools touched which shot, whether any voice is cloned, and whether the final file was re-encoded. It takes ten minutes and it is the document you will want if a platform queries a declaration.

Align the declaration with your on-screen label. If the video carries the disclosure ASCI expects, the upload declaration should say the same thing. The December requirements are in [our breakdown of the six cases that need an ASCI label](/blog/asci-ai-label-six-cases-december-2026).

The caveat: the rules do not define a threshold for "appears real", and none of the sources we read give rule numbers or penalties for advertisers specifically. Until platforms publish their own interpretation, declare when in doubt. Over-declaring costs you a label. Under-declaring costs you a takedown.
