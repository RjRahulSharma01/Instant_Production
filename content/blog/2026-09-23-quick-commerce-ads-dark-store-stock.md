A quick commerce ad can only sell a product that is sitting in the dark store serving that shopper. Blinkit ran 2,443 of those stores at the end of June 2026 and Instamart ran 1,171, and each one is its own shelf. Before you blame the creative or the bid for a weak Blinkit ROAS, check how many of those shelves actually had your SKU on the days you were paying for clicks.

That sounds obvious. In practice it is the most common reason we see quick commerce budgets underperform for D2C brands, and it rarely shows up in the dashboard the brand team looks at every week.

## The shelf is the store, not the city

On Blinkit, Zepto and Instamart a listing shows as available to a shopper only if stock is physically present in the dark store that serves their location. Units sitting in the platform's mother warehouse do not count. A shopper in one neighbourhood and a shopper three kilometres away can open the same app at the same minute and see two different catalogues.

Campaign setup, though, is mostly done at the city or keyword level. So the brand buys "Bengaluru, keyword: protein bar" and the platform serves that intent across every store in the city. Wherever your SKU is out, the best case is that the impression goes nowhere. The worst case is that you paid to put a shopper in front of an out-of-stock tile, and they bought the competitor sitting next to it.

Analytics firm 42Signals, which tracks dark store availability, put it bluntly in a June 2026 piece: a platform can report a brand as live in a city while it is missing from 40 percent of that city's dark stores. We have seen the same pattern in client data. National listing and actual availability are two different numbers.

## Why this matters more in 2026

Three things changed this year that make store-level availability the deciding variable.

### The networks got bigger and denser

Eternal's Q1 FY27 shareholder letter shows Blinkit added 200 net new stores in the June quarter, taking it to 2,443, up from 1,544 a year earlier. Average net order value per store per day was ₹8.27 lakh. Management raised its steady-state assumption to ₹11 lakh per store per day and its capex assumption per store, including warehousing, to ₹2.5 crore from ₹1 crore. Bigger stores with deeper assortment mean more SKUs competing for the same shelf, and more stores means more places for your stock to run thin.

Instamart ended the same quarter with 1,171 stores across 131 cities. Swiggy reported that more than 45 percent of those stores were contribution-margin positive, which tells you the platform now thinks about its business store by store. Brands should too.

### Blinkit buys your stock now

Blinkit has moved to an inventory-led model, buying product directly from brands and holding it in its own warehouses and stores. That puts replenishment decisions with the platform's category and supply teams, not with you. Your media plan and your purchase orders are now run by two different organisations with two different calendars. When a campaign spikes demand in a store the platform did not stock deeply, the ad keeps spending and the shelf goes empty.

### The platforms are optimising per order

Swiggy said Instamart's adjusted revenue per order rose to ₹108 in Q1 FY27 from ₹97 the previous quarter, and that it shed more than 4 million unprofitable users to get to contribution breakeven in May 2026. Eternal said Q1 FY27 was the peak of competitive intensity it had seen. When platforms are squeezing more revenue out of each order, ad inventory gets priced accordingly. Paying premium rates for impressions that land on empty shelves is the most expensive way to lose money on these apps.

Storyboard18 has reported projections that Blinkit, Zepto and Instamart together could earn close to ₹4,900 crore in ad revenue in 2026. A meaningful slice of that is being paid by brands against stores where they were not in stock.

> On quick commerce, availability is the first targeting parameter. Everything else in the campaign sits on top of it.

## What a store-level ROAS read looks like

Here is how the same campaign reads when you split it by availability. The numbers below are illustrative, built from the shape we typically see, not a single client's data.

| View | Spend share | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| City-level ROAS | 100% | A blended number that hides where money is wasted |
| Stores in stock all week | ~60% | The true return on the ad itself |
| Stores out of stock part of the week | ~25% | Return depressed by gaps, not by creative |
| Stores that never stocked the SKU | ~15% | Spend that could not have converted |

The first row is what most brand teams report. The second row is the only one that tells you whether the ad works. The last two rows are a supply problem wearing a media costume.

## What we do before scaling a quick commerce budget

This is the checklist we run with D2C clients before any increase in quick commerce spend.

1. **Get store-level availability data.** Either from the platform's brand portal where it is exposed, or from a digital shelf tracker. Daily, not weekly. A SKU that is out every Friday evening is invisible in a weekly average.
2. **Split ROAS by availability, not by keyword.** Report return only for store-days where the SKU was in stock. That is the number you optimise creative and bids against.
3. **Match budget to shelf depth.** Heavier spend in stores that carry deep stock of the hero SKU, lighter or paused spend where stock is thin. Where platform tools do not allow store-level targeting, use pincode or area targeting as the nearest proxy.
4. **Send the out-of-stock map to the category manager.** Store-level gap data is the strongest argument you have for a bigger allocation in specific stores. It turns a request into evidence.
5. **Plan promotions with supply, not after.** If a campaign or a sale event is coming, the replenishment has to land in stores before the spend starts. Running a discount push into empty shelves also creates problems under the new prior-price rules we covered in [our post on discount claims](/blog/discount-claim-prior-price-rule-2027).

None of this is exotic. It is the same discipline a modern trade team applies to a physical retail chain, applied to 2,000 small stores that restock several times a day.

## The honest caveat

Store-level data is uneven. Blinkit, Zepto and Instamart expose different levels of detail to brands, and third-party trackers infer availability rather than reading it from the platform's inventory system. Treat any single source as a strong signal, not an audit. And a clean availability picture will not rescue a product that shoppers do not want: once you have separated supply from media, some SKUs will simply show a poor return in fully stocked stores. That is useful to know too.

If you sell on quick commerce and cannot currently answer "what share of last month's ad spend ran in stores where we were out of stock," that is the first number to get. It usually changes the budget conversation more than any creative test.
