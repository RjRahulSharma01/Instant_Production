ASCI reviewed 40 summer ads between April and June 2026 and only one held up. Of the other 39, 21 were changed or pulled by the advertiser without a fight, and 18 were found in breach of the ASCI Code by its Consumer Complaints Council. Sun protection and tan removal was the biggest offending category. The pattern behind the numbers is simple: the claim went live before the evidence existed.

The findings are in ASCI's report "The Heat Is On: ASCI Review of Summer Advertising Claims", published in early August 2026. If you run a beauty or personal care brand, or the ads for one, this is the most useful document of the year to read against your own creative.

## What the 40 ads actually were

Read the number carefully before quoting it. These 40 were not a random sample of summer advertising. They were ads that ASCI picked up for review, so "97.5%" is not the share of all summer ads that break the rules. It tells you how badly claims fared once someone asked for proof.

By category, sun protection and tan removal products were the largest group, followed by home appliances (cooling, air purification, indoor comfort) and then personal care, wearables and travel products. The claims that failed were specific and familiar to anyone who has briefed a summer campaign:

- Instant beauty results, such as "de-tan in 30 seconds" and "visible results in 5 days"
- Technical ratings and percentages, such as UPF numbers and filtration figures, with no supporting data
- Market leadership claims with no basis a reader could check
- Health and wellness positioning built on weak or selective evidence

ASCI also flagged celebrity and influencer endorsements where the endorser had not been given, or had not checked, the evidence behind what they were saying.

## The 14 ads that went beyond weak evidence

Fourteen of the ads were skin-brightening or tan-removal products aimed at babies and children. ASCI's finding was that they reinforced colour-based stereotypes by playing on parental anxiety.

This is not a new rule being applied. ASCI released its guidelines on skin lightening and fairness products in August 2014, and they bar advertising that shows darker skin as inferior or unsuccessful, or ties skin tone to status, community or profession. What is new is the target: infants and children, where a parent is the buyer and the anxiety is the lever.

If your brief says "brighter skin for baby," that is a rewrite, not a tweak. Removing the word "fairness" while keeping the visual story of a child whose skin lightens does not fix it.

## Why the same mistake repeats every season

Most clients we work with hit this in the same place. The creative team writes the claim, the brand team likes it, and the proof is expected to turn up later from the manufacturer. In the ASCI report, non-response to substantiation requests is itself listed as a problem. An advertiser that cannot produce a test report within the response window has already lost the argument.

ASCI's CEO and Secretary General Manisha Kapoor put the standard plainly: claims made must be supported by credible evidence.

> A claim is not ready to run when the copy is approved. It is ready to run when the test report that backs it is in the same folder.

Seasonal categories make this worse. Summer launches are compressed into a few weeks, sunscreen and de-tan sit in a crowded shelf fight, and the temptation is to go with the boldest number the lab report will barely support. The number that survives is the one with a named test, a defined condition and a date.

## This is a pattern, not a one-off

The summer review lands four months after ASCI's April 2026 report on beauty and personal care, which flagged over 500 brands between 2025 and January 2026. In that coverage, ASCI's Kapoor said many cases involved exaggerated or inadequately substantiated claims on skin benefits, fairness, anti-ageing or treatment-like results.

That treatment-like wording connects to a separate risk. A cosmetic that promises to treat something can stop being treated as a cosmetic at all, which we covered in [our piece on CDSCO and cosmetic treatment claims](/blog/cdsco-cosmetic-treatment-claims-2026). Influencer disclosure is the other recurring failure in the same category, covered in [our beauty influencer disclosure post](/blog/beauty-influencer-disclosure).

## A pre-flight check for sun and skin claims

We run this check before any sunscreen, tan-removal or brightening creative goes into production. It takes about an hour.

| Claim type | What to have on file before it runs |
|---|---|
| Time-bound result ("in 30 seconds", "in 5 days") | Test protocol, sample size, conditions, and the timing basis |
| SPF, PA or UPF rating | The lab report for the exact formulation and pack being sold |
| "Number one" or leadership | The dated source, the category definition and the measure used |
| Before and after imagery | Confirmation nothing was retouched or lit differently |
| Endorser statement | The evidence pack the endorser was shown, and their sign-off |
| Anything aimed at infants or children | Legal review, not just a claims check |

Two habits make this stick. First, write the evidence reference next to each claim in the script, so the editor and the media buyer can see what each line rests on. Second, brief influencers with the evidence, not just the talking points, because ASCI now looks at whether the endorser was in a position to verify what they said.

## What to do this week

Pull every live sun protection, de-tan and brightening ad you have running and match each claim to a document. Pause anything where the match is missing, and fix the time-bound "instant" claims first, since they were the most commonly cited.

If you are planning the winter and festive skincare cycle, put the evidence file in the brief before the creative agency starts, not after.
