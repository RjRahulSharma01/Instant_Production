If your online-degree campaign says "in collaboration with a UGC-recognised university," UGC's position is that the line proves nothing. On 5 August 2026, the Ministry of Education told the Rajya Sabha that EdTech companies advertising ODL and online degree or diploma programmes through franchise arrangements with universities are running programmes UGC does not recognise — and that action will follow against both the EdTech company and the institution.

That last clause is the part most performance teams miss. The regulator is not only chasing the university. It named the EdTech company advertising the programme.

## What the rule actually says

The operative text sits in the UGC (Open and Distance Learning Programmes and Online Programmes) Regulations, 2020. Regulation 4(C)(vi) requires an institution applying to offer online programmes to show it has no franchise arrangement with a private service provider, and that ownership of every component of the programme rests with the institution alone. Learner support centres have to be run by the institution directly, not subcontracted.

So the compliance question is not "does this university exist and is it recognised." It is "is this specific programme, in this specific mode, entitled on the UGC-DEB list, and is the institution actually delivering it."

Those are different questions, and almost every ad we audit in this category answers only the first one.

## The claim structure that creates the problem

Education ad copy rarely lies outright. It borrows credibility through adjacency. Four patterns do most of the damage:

- The university logo placed next to the platform logo, with no statement of who awards the degree.
- "UGC-recognised university" as a standalone badge, when what needs recognising is the programme.
- "Approved," "entitled," and "accredited" used interchangeably in the same funnel — they mean three different things and only one of them applies to ODL/online mode.
- Placement or salary outcomes attributed to the degree, sourced from the platform's own cohort data.

| What the ad says | What a reader assumes | What UGC checks |
|---|---|---|
| "In collaboration with XYZ University" | XYZ awards the degree | Whether XYZ is entitled for that programme in ODL/online mode |
| "UGC-recognised university" | The course is recognised | Programme-level entitlement on the UGC-DEB list |
| "Industry-designed curriculum by [platform]" | Better course | Whether curriculum ownership sits outside the HEI — which breaks 4(C)(vi) |

> The moment a private platform owns the curriculum, the delivery, or the learner support, the degree stops being the university's to award — and the ad stops being defensible.

## The enforcement picture is wider than one notice

UGC's Anti-Malpractice Cell, set up on 30 May 1996, handles unauthorised institutions awarding degrees in contravention of the UGC Act, 1956. Its February 2026 list names 32 institutions as fake universities. UGC has also asked state governments and union territories to register FIRs against such entities where warranted.

Separately, the Central Consumer Protection Authority's 2024 guidelines on misleading advertising in the coaching sector already established that education advertisers carry disclosure obligations independent of any sectoral regulator. An education advertiser in India now sits under at least three overlapping regimes: UGC for what a degree is, CCPA for what an ad may claim, and ASCI's code for how the claim is presented. Most clients we work with have a legal review process for one of the three.

## What we change in the account

We treat programme entitlement as a creative input, not a legal afterthought. Concretely:

**Verify at the programme level before the brief is written.** Pull the entitlement status from the UGC-DEB site for the exact programme, exact mode, exact academic session. If it is not there, the campaign does not run the degree claim — it runs a certificate or upskilling claim, which is a different and entirely legitimate product.

**Separate the award from the delivery in the copy.** Say plainly who confers the degree and what the platform provides. "Degree awarded by X University. Learning support and career services by [platform]." It is duller and it survives scrutiny.

**Kill the badge, keep the link.** Replace "UGC-recognised" wordmarks in creative with a verifiable line on the landing page pointing to the DEB entitlement record. Anyone building education funnels should read this alongside our note on [coaching ad claims under CCPA](/blog/coaching-ad-claims-ccpa) — the disclosure logic is the same.

**Audit the affiliate layer.** Most of the offending copy we find is not on the brand's own account. It is on counsellor WhatsApp forwards, affiliate landing pages, and lead-gen microsites that no one at the company has read. Those still carry the brand's name, and consent captured on them still falls under the DPDP obligations we covered in [consent and first-party data](/blog/dpdp-consent-first-party-data).

## Why this lands on the media buyer's desk

The instinct is to route this to legal and keep spending. It does not work that way, because the exposure sits in the creative, not the contract. UGC's notice describes advertisements in newspapers, on social media, and on television as the trigger — the thing that made the arrangement visible and actionable. A perfectly-drafted MoU with a bad hook line still produces a public notice.

There is also a practical cost. Degree claims carry the cheapest CPLs in this category precisely because they promise the most, so cutting them looks like a performance decision rather than a compliance one. In our experience the drop is smaller than teams fear and the lead quality is better: people who convert on a clearly-scoped certificate offer do not churn out at the fee-payment stage when they discover what they actually bought.

Build the honest version of the ad first, then find out what it costs. That order saves the argument later.

## The honest caveat

Not every EdTech-university partnership is a franchise arrangement. The regulations permit institutions to buy technology, hosting, and marketing services. The line is ownership: who designs the curriculum, who assesses, who supports learners, who awards. A vendor relationship is fine. A programme that would collapse if the platform walked away is not.

If you are running education acquisition and cannot say, in one sentence, which entity awards the qualification your ads are selling, stop the spend and check the DEB list first. That check takes an afternoon. Rebuilding a lead pipeline after a public notice takes a quarter — we have watched it happen in [other regulated categories](/industries/education) and the pattern never varies.
