Social media influencers accounted for 68% of the personal care advertisements ASCI processed, and 92% of those required modification. More than 500 brands were flagged for advertising breaches between 2025 and January 2026. The most common failure is not an exaggerated claim about the product. It is that the post never clearly said it was paid for.

That is a fixable problem. It costs nothing, it takes one line in a brief, and the brands topping the violation list are not small operations who did not know better.

## Who is on the list

[Honasa Consumer, the parent of Mamaearth, was the most flagged mainstream beauty company](https://www.storyboard18.com/advertising/honasa-loreal-hul-top-ascis-influencer-disclosure-violations-list-in-fy26-in-personal-care-99540.htm) for influencer disclosure violations in FY26, with 24 cases requiring modification. L'Oréal followed with 17, Hindustan Unilever with 12, MARS Cosmetics with 7 and Nykaa with 5.

These are companies with legal departments. The violations are not happening because nobody knows the rule. They are happening because influencer marketing at scale is run by a coordinator with a spreadsheet of two hundred creators, and nobody checks two hundred posts.

The [breaches span three types](https://www.storyboard18.com/brand-marketing/india-cracks-down-on-beauty-brands-for-misleading-influencer-ads-ws-l-93976.htm): exaggerated product claims, misleading use of words like "natural" and "Ayurvedic", and undisclosed paid partnerships. The third is the volume driver.

## What disclosure actually requires

ASCI requires paid collaborations to be labelled with an unmissable identifier: "Ad", "Sponsored", "Paid partnership", "Collaboration". The requirement is not just that the word appears. It is that a viewer sees it while consuming the content.

Which rules out most of what brands currently do.

| Common practice | Why it fails |
| --- | --- |
| `#ad` as the fourteenth hashtag | Not prominent. Nobody reads past the third |
| Disclosure in the caption on a Reel | Most viewers never expand the caption |
| "Thanks to @brand for sending this" | Gifting is still a material connection and still needs disclosing |
| Disclosure in the video at 0:48 | Has to be at the start, not when the pitch arrives |
| Platform tag only, no on-screen label | Better than nothing, still not prominent for a scrolling viewer |

The version that works: the label in the first line of the caption, and on-screen in the first three seconds of video. It takes one sentence in the brief and one round of checking before the post goes live.

Brands resist this because they believe disclosure suppresses performance. In our experience it does not, materially, and the effect it does have is concentrated in exactly the audience that was never going to buy on the strength of a hidden ad. Indian audiences correctly assume most creator content is paid. Disclosure confirms what they already think and removes the thing that makes them feel cheated when they find out.

> Gifting counts. If you sent the product free, that is a material connection, and every "look what arrived!" unboxing without a label is a violation your legal team has not been told about.

## The claims problem underneath

Disclosure is the easy half. The harder half is that a creator will say things about your product that you would never put in your own advertising, because they are speaking casually and because you briefed them on benefits rather than on limits.

"Cleared my acne in a week." "Completely natural." "Better than any serum I've used." The first is a therapeutic claim, the second is a term ASCI is actively policing in this category, the third is unsubstantiated superiority. None of it appears on your packaging. All of it appears in your campaign.

The mechanism that prevents this is boring: a one-page list of what cannot be said, sent with every brief, plus copy approval before posting for anything above a certain follower count. Micro-creators posting at volume can be handled with the do-not-say list alone. Anyone whose post will be amplified with paid spend should go through approval, because the moment you put media behind it, it is your advertisement in every meaningful sense.

## Why micro-influencers changed the maths

The category has moved decisively toward creators in the 10,000 to 100,000 follower range, and the reason is not just cost. It is regional reach and the fact that a recommendation from someone who feels like a real person in your city outperforms a celebrity endorsement for a ₹499 serum.

The operational consequence is scale. A campaign that used to be six creators is now sixty, or two hundred. Compliance that relied on someone eyeballing each post does not survive that transition, which is precisely why the violation numbers look the way they do.

If you are running at that volume, you need process rather than vigilance: the do-not-say list in every brief, disclosure requirements stated as a deliverable rather than a request, a spot check of a random 10% of live posts each week, and a contract term that makes a non-disclosing post the creator's problem to fix within 24 hours.

## Turning it into media, properly

The other half of this is what happens after the post performs. Amplifying creator content through your own ad account, or whitelisting so it runs from theirs, is where influencer budgets start earning their keep, and it is where rights get messy.

Usage rights, duration, whitelisting permission and paid amplification are separate things and each needs to be in the agreement before the shoot, not negotiated after a post takes off. [Turning influencer content into paid](/blog/influencer-content-into-paid) covers what that agreement needs to contain.

Once it is running as paid media, it fatigues like any other creative, and the same refresh arithmetic applies, and [the creative testing loop](/blog/creative-testing-loop) is the relevant read there.

## When a notice actually arrives

Worth knowing the shape of this before it happens, because the instinct is usually wrong.

An ASCI complaint is not a court proceeding. The usual outcome is a request to modify or withdraw the advertisement, and most cases resolve there. Which is exactly why the numbers look the way they do. 92% requiring modification sounds catastrophic and mostly means a lot of posts got a label added or a claim softened.

The instinct is to quietly delete the post and hope. That is the wrong move for two reasons. Deleting destroys your own record of what was published, and it does not stop the case. Screenshot it, keep it, then respond.

What actually helps is being able to show process: the brief that contained the do-not-say list, the approval trail, the contract term requiring disclosure. A brand that can demonstrate it briefed correctly and had a creator go off-script is in a different position from one that cannot produce a brief at all.

The reputational cost is real but smaller than founders fear. The compounding cost is different. Repeat appearances on a violation list, in a category where the regulator has started publishing names, is what turns a marketing problem into a board conversation. Honasa's 24 cases are not 24 disasters. They are one process failure, twenty-four times.

## What to do this month

Audit thirty live posts across your current roster. Count how many carry a prominent disclosure. If it is under half, you are roughly at category average, which is not a comfort.

Rewrite the brief template to put disclosure and the do-not-say list above the creative direction, because that is the order of importance and briefs are read top-down.

Add copy approval for anyone you intend to amplify with paid spend.

And decide who owns this. In most beauty brands the answer today is nobody. The agency assumes the brand checks, the brand assumes the agency does, and 92% of the output needs modification.
