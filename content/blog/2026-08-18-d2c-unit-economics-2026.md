Indian D2C is growing quickly and getting harder to make money in at the same time. Meta acquisition cost has risen from roughly ₹380 to ₹502 in a year, a 32% jump. Quick commerce takes 30-40% of the order value but hands you zero acquisition cost. Cash on delivery is still 45% of orders and still a working capital drag. None of these is fatal on its own. Stacked, they decide whether a brand compounds or quietly stalls at ₹5 crore.

The category itself is not the problem. The Indian D2C market is [growing at about 24% a year and quick commerce considerably faster than that](https://www.mordorintelligence.com/industry-reports/india-d2c-ecommerce-market), with q-commerce now doing ₹40,000 crore-plus in annual GMV. Demand is there. Margin is where it is going wrong.

## The four costs that decide it

Most D2C P&Ls stop at gross margin, which is why so many founders are surprised by their bank balance. The number that actually governs the business is what is left after acquisition, delivery, returns and platform commission.

Take a ₹1,000 order at 60% gross margin. That is ₹600 before anything else happens.

Subtract acquisition. If blended CAC is ₹502 on Meta and you are acquiring a first-time customer, ₹600 becomes ₹98. Subtract shipping and packaging, call it ₹80 to ₹120 across most categories, and you are at or below zero on the first order. That is not a failing brand — that is a normal Indian D2C brand, and the whole model rests on the second and third purchase.

Now add returns. RTO on prepaid orders is manageable. RTO on cash on delivery is not, and COD is still nearly half of orders. A returned COD order costs you both legs of shipping and returns inventory you have already paid to pack.

This is why [contribution margin after delivery](https://aimnlaunch.com/cm2-for-ecommerce-the-only-profitability-metric-d2c-founders-in-india-should-track/), not gross margin, is the number to run the business on. Gross margin flatters an Indian D2C P&L because it ignores exactly the costs that are rising fastest.

## Quick commerce is a different business wearing the same clothes

The temptation is obvious. Blinkit, Zepto and Instamart hand you demand you did not have to buy. Zero CAC, zero shipping cost to you, immediate volume.

The price is 30-40% commission and the loss of the customer relationship. You do not get the email. You do not get the repeat purchase on your own terms. You get an order, a margin haircut, and a platform that now knows your sell-through better than you do.

| | Your own site | Quick commerce |
|---|---|---|
| Acquisition cost | ₹400-600 and rising | Effectively nil |
| Commission | Payment gateway only | 30-40% of order value |
| Shipping | Yours | Theirs |
| Customer data | Yours | Theirs |
| Repeat purchase | You can earn it | You can hope for it |
| Working capital | COD drag | Settlement cycle |

Neither column is the right answer on its own. The brands doing well run both and know which SKUs belong where — high-frequency, low-consideration, impulse-adjacent products on quick commerce; higher-ticket, considered, bundle-friendly products on their own site where the margin justifies the acquisition cost.

The mistake is treating quick commerce as a growth channel while still measuring the business on blended revenue. Blended revenue goes up. Contribution margin goes down. The dashboard says the quarter was good.

> If you cannot say which of your SKUs makes money on quick commerce and which one is buying you volume at a loss, you are not running two channels. You are running one channel and subsidising it with another.

## Where CAC is actually beatable

Top performers achieve [40-50% lower CAC than the category average](https://cms.growwwtech.com/state-of-indian-d2c-2026-industry-report/), and the difference is not a smarter bidding strategy. It comes from three unglamorous places.

**Creative volume.** In an auction where targeting is largely automated, creative is the main remaining variable. A brand shipping twenty-five assets a month against a brand shipping four is not slightly ahead, it is operating a different machine. This is the single highest-leverage change available to most brands and the one most consistently underfunded. The arithmetic is in [the creative testing loop](/blog/creative-testing-loop).

**Organic presence that does the pre-selling.** A customer who has read something of yours, or seen you three times on a creator's feed, converts at a materially better rate and costs less to convert. Organic is not free, it is just paid for earlier. Brands that started this in 2024 now have a cost structure their competitors cannot match by bidding harder.

**Conversion rate, which is nobody's job.** A 1.4% to 2.0% conversion improvement is a 30% CAC reduction, and it is usually sitting in page speed, a shorter checkout, and not asking for a pincode before showing the price. Most brands would rather run a new campaign than fix a checkout, because the campaign is more interesting.

## Cash on delivery, still

Down from 55% to 45% of orders, which is progress, and still enormous. Every COD order is inventory that ships before it is paid for, at a meaningful risk of coming back.

The levers that work: partial prepaid incentives sized to actual RTO cost rather than a round-number discount, address verification before dispatch on high-risk pincodes, and simply not offering COD on first orders above a threshold. Each of these costs some conversion. Each of them costs less than the RTO it prevents. Run the numbers on your own pincode data rather than a benchmark — RTO is intensely local and a Tier-2 average tells you nothing about your Tier-2.

## Retention is the only lever with compounding returns

Everything above is about defending margin on the first order. The business is decided on the second and third.

At a ₹502 acquisition cost and ₹98 of contribution on order one, a customer who buys twice is worth roughly ₹698 in contribution against the same ₹502. A customer who buys four times is worth ₹1,898 against ₹502. Nothing you do to bidding produces that kind of change. The industry-wide shift from growth-at-all-costs to unit economics is really a shift in where attention goes: from the first purchase to the fourth.

What actually drives repeat in Indian D2C, in rough order of effect:

- **Product consumption cycle.** If your product lasts ninety days, your reorder window is ninety days, and a campaign on day thirty is noise. Most brands do not know their actual consumption cycle and use a generic thirty-day flow.
- **Delivery experience on order one.** RTO and delayed delivery kill repeat harder than any discount fixes it.
- **Owned channel that is actually owned.** WhatsApp opt-in beats email in India by a wide margin, and both beat hoping they come back.
- **Assortment.** A single-SKU brand has a structural ceiling on repeat that no amount of retention marketing solves.

The honest test: pull your ninety-day repeat rate by acquisition cohort, by channel. If quick commerce customers never return to your site and never appear in a second cohort, that channel is a revenue line, not a customer acquisition line, and it should be budgeted as one.

## What to change this quarter

Rebuild your reporting around contribution margin after delivery, per SKU and per channel. Not blended. Blended is how brands discover a problem two quarters late.

Then decide, explicitly, which SKUs are on quick commerce to make money and which are there to buy shelf presence — and put a budget on the second group so it is a decision rather than a leak.

Raise creative output before raising spend. Spending more into fatigued creative raises CPM and teaches you nothing.

And look at your prepaid share honestly. Ten points of COD converted to prepaid is usually worth more than the campaign you were about to launch.
