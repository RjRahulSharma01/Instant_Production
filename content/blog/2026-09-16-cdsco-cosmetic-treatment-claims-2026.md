On 18 May 2026 CDSCO issued a public notice, File No. COS-1211/2026-eoffice, restating one boundary in plain language: a cosmetic is something rubbed, poured, sprinkled or sprayed on the body to cleanse, beautify, promote attractiveness or alter appearance. Nothing else. The moment your ad copy says a product treats, heals or corrects a condition, you have described a drug, and a drug you do not have a licence for.

That is not a new rule. It is Section 3(aaa) of the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940, read with Rule 36 of the Cosmetics Rules, 2020, which bars any cosmetic from conveying an idea that is false or misleading. What changed in May is that the regulator put it in writing, aimed it at aesthetic clinics and online sellers, and named the two behaviours it is watching: injection, and treatment claims.

## What the notice actually says

Four things, and the marketing consequences differ for each.

First, injectable preparations are not cosmetics. No cosmetic may be administered by injection, by a consumer, a professional or an aesthetic clinic. Skin boosters, filler-style injectables and anything else that breaks the skin sits under drug regulation, not cosmetic registration.

Second, cosmetics may not be used for therapeutic or medical treatment, by professionals or individuals.

Third, no cosmetic label may convey a false or misleading claim, and nobody may alter or deface a manufacturer's inscription on the container, label or wrapper.

Fourth, ingredients on the BIS list of substances generally not recognised as safe, and restricted ingredients under the Ninth Schedule, are out.

> The line CDSCO drew is not between strong claims and weak ones. It is between changing how skin looks and changing what skin is doing.

## Why this hits ad copy harder than packaging

Most brands we work with have their carton text lawyered. The label went through a regulatory consultant before the first print run. The ad copy did not.

That gap is where the exposure sits, because performance creative is written weekly by people optimising for click-through, not classification. A hook that tests well says "clears acne in 14 days." A compliant version says "helps reduce the look of blemishes." The first one is a treatment claim on an unlicensed product. The second one is a cosmetic claim. On a Meta feed they look like the same sentence.

The clinic category is worse, because the ad and the service are the same sale. A studio running lead-gen for skin boosters is now advertising a procedure that, per this notice, cannot legally be performed with a cosmetic-registered product at all.

## The verb test we run before anything ships

We look at this every time we write beauty copy: circle every verb, then ask which category each one belongs to.

| Verb in the copy | Reads as | Safe for a cosmetic ad |
| --- | --- | --- |
| cleanses, hydrates, smoothes, brightens the look of | Appearance | Yes |
| minimises the appearance of, visibly evens | Appearance | Yes |
| treats, cures, heals, repairs, corrects | Therapy | No |
| clinically proven, dermatologist recommended | Evidence claim | Only with the actual study or the actual recommendation on file |
| injected, infused under the skin, in-clinic shot | Drug administration | No |

The middle row is where people get caught, and the fourth row is where they get caught twice. The CCPA's 2022 Guidelines for Prevention of Misleading Advertisements require that any claim relating to an objectively verifiable fact be capable of substantiation, and they explicitly say a disclaimer cannot rescue a claim that was not true to begin with. The example the Guidelines themselves give is a skin cream advertising "dermatologists recommend this product for all skin types" with a small-font "individual results may vary" underneath. That does not work.

## The compliance numbers are not comforting

ASCI's 2023-24 Annual Complaints Report found that 98 percent of scrutinised beauty advertisements required modification. Its 2024-25 report processed 9,599 complaints at an average resolution time of 16 days, with overall compliance at 83 percent, television and print near 98 percent, and digital lagging at 75 percent.

Read those two figures together. Beauty is the category most likely to need a fix, and digital is the medium least likely to comply. If you are a D2C skincare brand running most of your spend on Meta and Google, you are sitting at the intersection of both.

ASCI rulings are advisory rather than legally binding. The CCPA's are not: Section 89 of the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 allows penalties up to ₹10 lakh for a first contravention and up to ₹50 lakh for repeat ones, plus a bar on the endorser for up to one year, extendable to three. So the ASCI notice is usually the cheap warning. The expensive one comes later.

## What to do this week

Pull your live creative, not your brand book. Export every active ad copy variant from the last 90 days into one sheet and run the verb test down the column. In most accounts we audit, the offending lines are in a handful of high-spend evergreen ads that nobody has reopened since they started working.

Then check three places people forget. Landing page H1s, which are frequently written by a different person than the ad. Influencer scripts, where a creator paraphrases "reduces the appearance of pigmentation" into "it removed my pigmentation" and the brand inherits the claim. And your own Instagram captions, which are advertisements in law even though they feel like posts.

For anything you want to keep saying, find the evidence or change the verb. If you have a consumer perception study showing 8 in 10 users saw visible improvement, say exactly that, with the sample size in the same font. If you have nothing, describe the mechanism instead of promising the outcome. Naming the ingredient and what it does is almost always available to you when the claim is not.

The honest caveat: this notice does not create a positive list of permitted claims the way Japan's 56-claim framework does, so there is still no bright line telling you that a specific phrase is safe. Until India publishes cosmetics-specific claim guidance, you are working against a definition, not a checklist. That makes documentation your real defence. Keep the substantiation file for every claim, dated, alongside the creative it belongs to, so that when someone asks why you said it, the answer exists before the question does.
