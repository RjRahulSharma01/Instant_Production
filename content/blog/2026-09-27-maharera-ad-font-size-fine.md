Most developers think they are covered on RERA advertising once the QR code and registration number are somewhere on the creative. MahaRERA's own enforcement record says otherwise. Under Order No. 46C of 2025, the registration number and the MahaRERA website address must appear in a font size equal to or larger than the largest font used for the project's contact details. A Pune based developer found that out when MahaRERA fined the firm for printing its registration number smaller than its own phone number, even though the QR code itself was present and scannable.

## What the order actually requires

The underlying obligation to display a QR code, the registration number and the MahaRERA website link on every advertisement has been in place since August 2023, made mandatory under Section 3 read with Sections 63 and 65 of the Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016. Section 3 pulls in any project on land over 500 square metres or with eight or more apartments across all phases, which covers the large majority of branded residential launches in the state.

What changed in 2025 is enforcement detail. MahaRERA had observed that promoters were technically complying by tucking the QR code into a corner too small to scan, or by printing the registration number in a font so faint it needed a magnifying glass next to a bold, oversized sales number. Order No. 46C closes that gap directly: the QR code goes in the top right corner in a clearly visible, scannable size, and the registration number and website address cannot be smaller than the biggest font on the page used for contact information. This applies to print, hoardings, brochures, websites and social media alike, and it applies whether the post was paid media or an organic Instagram carousel the sales team put up for free.

## The penalty ladder

MahaRERA does not treat every violation as a flat fine. The structure escalates if a developer sits on it.

| Stage | Amount | Trigger |
|---|---|---|
| Initial penalty | Rs 15,000 | First finding of non-compliance under Section 63 |
| Deposit window | 15 days | Time given to pay before escalation |
| Escalated penalty | Doubles, capped at Rs 50,000 | Penalty unpaid after the 15 day window |

The Pune case that surfaced this year followed exactly that path: a Rs 15,000 penalty for a font size gap between the registration number and the contact details, not for an absent QR code or a fake registration number. That distinction matters more than most creative teams assume, because it means a technically present disclosure can still fail the rule on formatting alone.

> The rule was never really about whether a QR code exists on the page. It is about whether a buyer's eye reaches the registration number before it reaches the phone number.

## Why this trips up agencies more than developers

Most of the real estate creative we review at the layout stage treats RERA disclosures as a legal footer problem: get the number on the page, get the QR code somewhere, move on. That approach worked when enforcement was sparse. It does not work against a rule that measures font points against font points on the same asset.

We now run every real estate print and digital layout through a specific check before it goes to media buying: measure the point size of the registration number and website URL against the largest point size anywhere in the contact block, on the actual production file, not the design mockup. A brochure that passes review at draft stage can still fail once a printer's imposition process rescales elements unevenly, so the check has to happen on the final output, not the layout draft.

Social media adds its own failure point. A creative that is compliant as a static JPEG can lose its QR code entirely once a platform crops it for a Story or a Reel cover frame. Anyone running organic real estate content through Instagram or Facebook needs to confirm the QR code and registration text survive every crop the platform applies, not just the master file.

## What this means for the next launch

Three checks are worth building into a pre-launch sign-off rather than trusting the design file:

First, confirm the project actually needs registration under Section 3's thresholds before assuming the rule applies at all; a small independent villa project under 500 square metres and fewer than eight units may sit outside RERA's registration requirement entirely, though most brand launches will not.

Second, measure font sizes on the final production asset across every format it will run in, print, web banner, hoarding and social crop, since MahaRERA's enforcement so far has targeted exactly this formatting gap rather than missing disclosures outright.

Third, keep a dated screenshot or print proof of every published creative. The Pune order shows MahaRERA acting on individual advertisements, not just registration filings, so the evidence a developer needs to defend a placement is the actual published creative, not the approved design brief.

None of this requires new legal advice for most standard residential launches. It requires the same production discipline agencies already apply to logo usage or brand colour, just aimed at a compliance element that most creative workflows still treat as an afterthought. Developers advertising outside Maharashtra should check their own state RERA's advertisement circulars separately: Tamil Nadu, for instance, runs its own penalty schedule for non-compliant property ads, and the requirements are not identical state to state.
