If you sell a supplement, a protein blend or an ayurvedic wellness SKU in India, two lines in your current ad copy are already non-compliant and have been for months. The first is any standalone use of "100%". The second is any ashwagandha claim that doesn't specify root. Neither needs a new law to bite — both were settled by advisories that are already in force.

Most wellness brands we work with found out the same way: a packaging refresh, a legal review before a funding round, or a marketplace listing takedown. Almost nobody found out from their agency.

## The "100%" advisory is not a labelling rule

On 28 May 2025, FSSAI issued an advisory (File No. RCD-02001/133/2024-Regulatory-FSSAI) directing all Food Business Operators to discontinue the use of "100%" on labels, packaging **and promotional material**. That last phrase is the one performance teams keep missing. It covers your Meta primary text, your Google RSA headlines, your Amazon A+ content and your influencer scripts, not just the pack.

FSSAI's reasoning is narrow and worth understanding, because it tells you what survives. The regulator's position is that "100%" is not defined anywhere in the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006 or the regulations under it. With no definition, the term has no substantiable meaning — and Sub-regulation 4(1) of the Food Safety and Standards (Advertising and Claims) Regulations, 2018 requires every claim to be truthful, unambiguous, meaningful and not misleading. Sub-regulation 10(7) separately bars advertising that undermines other manufacturers or shapes consumer perception misleadingly. An unqualified "100% pure" implies the shelf next to you is not.

What survives is a qualified, factual statement about a specific component. "Made with 100% whole grain oats" is a checkable fact about an ingredient. "100% pure protein" is a superiority claim dressed as a spec.

> The problem was never the number. It was using an absolute about the product to say something about the competition.

## Ashwagandha is now a plant-part question

In April 2026, the Ministry of AYUSH issued an advisory permitting only the root of *Withania somnifera* in crude material, extracts and compound products. Leaves are prohibited in any form, on the basis of safety concerns around higher Withaferin A levels. AYUSH shared the advisory with FSSAI, which makes it directly relevant to every nutraceutical and health supplement SKU.

For a marketing team this converts into a copy problem overnight. Product pages that say "full-spectrum ashwagandha" or "whole plant extract" are now advertising an ingredient the brand should not be using. The Food Safety and Standards (Health Supplements, Nutraceuticals, Food for Special Dietary Use, Food for Special Medical Purpose and Prebiotic and Probiotic Food) Regulations, 2016 require ingredients to be safe, scientifically validated and accurately described — the plant part is part of the description, not a detail.

We look at this every time we onboard an ayurveda or adaptogen client now: pull every SKU's ingredient line, then pull every ad and landing page that mentions the ingredient, and check whether the two agree on plant part. They usually don't, because the ad copy was written from a mood board and the formulation sheet was written by a lab.

## What changes in the ad account, specifically

| Copy in market today | Status | Compliant replacement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "100% natural", "100% pure" | Covered by the 28 May 2025 advisory | Name the actual attribute: "no added preservatives" |
| "100% fruit juice" | Explicitly directed for removal | State composition per the label |
| "Full-spectrum ashwagandha" | Conflicts with the April 2026 AYUSH advisory | "Ashwagandha root extract" |
| "Boosts immunity", "reduces cholesterol" | Therapeutic claim, outside the food claims framework | Describe the nutrient and its declared level |
| Fruit name on a product with no fruit | Misleading nomenclature under the 2018 Regulations | Rename or carry the front-of-pack disclaimer |

The therapeutic-claim row is the oldest of these and still the most common. A food or supplement claim about preventing, treating or curing a condition is not a claim FSSAI can approve — it belongs to a different statute entirely.

## Why this shows up as a media problem before a legal one

Non-compliant claim copy rarely arrives as a notice first. It arrives as disapprovals. Meta and Google both run health and supplement claim classifiers that flag absolute and therapeutic language, and the practical effect is uneven: one ad set clears, its near-identical variant doesn't, and delivery on the winning creative gets throttled while the account picks up policy strikes.

Teams then read that as creative fatigue and rebuild the creative. The rebuild inherits the same claim line, because the claim line lives in the brief, not the ad. Three cycles later the brand has spent a month of budget re-testing a sentence that was never going to be allowed to scale.

The cheap fix is to move claims out of the creative brief and into a separate approved-claims document that the formulation lead signs off. Every headline, hook and creator script draws from that list. It sounds bureaucratic; it takes about two hours to build and it removes an entire class of disapproval from your [performance marketing](/services/performance-marketing) workflow.

## The 2027 date you should plan artwork around

The Food Safety and Standards (Labelling and Display) Amendment Regulations, 2026 were notified on 24 March 2026 and come into force on 1 July 2027. They change fortification declarations ("Fortified with", plus the prescribed logo), exempt packs under 100 sq cm from displaying the FSSAI logo, tighten non-retail container marking, and update artificial-sweetener declarations.

That is a packaging cycle, not a campaign cycle. If you are already planning a rebrand or a pack refresh for FY27, fold the labelling amendment into that brief now rather than paying for two artwork rounds. The claim clean-up, by contrast, is live today and doesn't need to wait for anything.

## Where to start this week

Export every active ad, listing and landing page for your food and supplement SKUs — including [marketplace listings](/industries/ecommerce), which are usually the most stale — and run a plain text search for "100%", "pure", "natural", "boosts", "cures", "prevents" and "full-spectrum". That single pass finds most of the exposure in under an hour.

One honest caveat: the "100%" instruction is an advisory, not a gazetted amendment, and advisories are enforced through the existing sub-regulations rather than a standalone penalty clause. That does not make it optional — FSSAI has been issuing notices citing exactly these provisions, typically with a seven-day window to respond — but it does mean your legal counsel, not this article, should decide how aggressively you retire in-market packaging versus digital copy. Digital copy is the free half. Fix that first.
