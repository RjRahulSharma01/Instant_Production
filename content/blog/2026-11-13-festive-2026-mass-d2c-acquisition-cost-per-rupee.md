The Diwali 2026 numbers are in, and the headline for D2C is the wrong one to plan from. Customer acquisition cost rose 15 to 20%, to roughly ₹600 to ₹650, according to Kearney. But for mass-market brands whose baskets shrank about 3%, Growster's calculations put the rise in acquisition cost per rupee of sales at 19 to 24%. Premium brands, with baskets up about 14%, saw only 1 to 5%. Same auction, very different season.

If you run a sub-₹1,000 catalogue, that gap is the number to take into your wedding-season plan, which starts on 21 November.

## What the festive data actually says

Several datasets landed this week, and they agree on direction even where they differ on size.

Unicommerce, working from more than 138 million order items processed on its Uniware platform between 6 October and 4 November, found orders through D2C brand websites up 30% and their GMV up 26%. Marketplaces grew orders 8% and value 6%, though they still carry the largest share of volume. Quick commerce was the fastest channel, with orders up 75%. And 85% of all festive orders were valued under ₹1,000.

Datum Intelligence's Festive Barometer 2026, reported by Business Standard, put total festive online sales at ₹1,40,800 crore over 34 sale days, up 17% on 2025 but short of its own ₹1,50,000 to ₹1,55,000 crore outlook. Its summary line is the one D2C teams should pin to the wall: the festival reached further and spent less per order.

ClickPost's cohort of 200 D2C brands (each with 500+ forward orders in both comparison windows) showed shipments up 31.4% in the opening days. The median brand, though, grew about 19%, and more than one in five brands saw shipments fall by over 25%.

## Why a 17% CAC rise becomes a 21% problem

The reason mass brands got hit harder is plain arithmetic, and it is worth doing yourself rather than taking anyone's percentage on trust.

Acquisition cost per rupee of sales is just CAC divided by the first-order value. If CAC goes up and the basket goes down, the two effects multiply. If CAC goes up and the basket also goes up, they partly cancel.

| | Mass-market D2C | Premium D2C |
|---|---|---|
| CAC change (Kearney range) | +15 to 20% | +15 to 20% |
| Basket change (Growster) | about -3% | about +14% |
| CAC per rupee of sales | +19 to 24% | +1 to 5% |
| What it feels like | Every order works harder to pay back | Roughly last year's economics |

Run it: 1.15 divided by 0.97 is about 1.19, and 1.20 divided by 0.97 is about 1.24. On the premium side, 1.15 divided by 1.14 is about 1.01, and 1.20 divided by 1.14 is about 1.05. Growster's ranges fall straight out of the two inputs. Nothing exotic is happening; a smaller basket simply has less room to absorb a pricier click.

> A rising CAC is survivable. A rising CAC on a shrinking basket is a margin problem wearing a media-buying costume.

This is why we push clients to report CAC as a share of first-order contribution margin, not as a rupee figure. A ₹640 CAC is fine on a ₹2,400 skincare kit and fatal on an ₹870 COD order, which, per ClickPost, was roughly the median COD basket in its cohort this season (prepaid sat at ₹1,044).

## Where the money went instead

Two shifts in spend explain part of why the auction got more expensive for brands bidding on broad festive intent.

Datum Intelligence forecast commerce and retail media ad spend growing 27% this season, against 13 to 17% for festive advertising overall. More budget moving into marketplace and quick-commerce ad units means more competition for the shopper who is already in a buying session, and it pulls bigger brands' attention (and money) into placements a pure website-led D2C brand cannot buy.

On creators, Qoruz data cited in the round-up shows the top 10 to 15% of brands took nearly 65% of festive creator budgets. The useful detail is the second one: 51% of festive creator collaborations happened outside Diwali week. The brands that did well on creator content did not wait for the most expensive seven days to show up.

## The geography and payment mix changed the basket

Tier 3 towns accounted for 41% of festive orders in Unicommerce's data, growing 26%, and 45% of D2C shipments in ClickPost's cohort. Tier 1 and metros grew orders only 13%.

That is good news for reach and less good news for basket size. Smaller-town shoppers skew to lower first orders, and they are the reason a lot of mass brands saw volume go up while revenue per order went down.

The payment shift is the partial offset. Prepaid accounted for 61% of festive orders in Unicommerce's numbers, and in ClickPost's cohort prepaid shipments grew 47% against 27% for COD, taking prepaid to 52% of shipments from 46%. Since Unicommerce's FY2026 data showed 58% of COD orders coming back during the festive quarter versus under 15% of prepaid, every point of prepaid share recovers some of the CAC you lost to the smaller basket. If you are not already measuring CAC on delivered orders rather than placed orders, this season is the argument for starting.

## What we would change before the wedding season

The wedding window opens on 21 November. Here is what we are doing for the D2C accounts we run, and what we would suggest to anyone with a mass catalogue.

### Raise the first basket on purpose

The cheapest lever on per-rupee CAC is the denominator. Bundles, a free-shipping threshold set just above your median order, and "complete the routine" add-ons on the PDP all move the first order without touching the bid. If your median basket is ₹870, a ₹999 threshold is not a discount; it is a CAC fix.

### Bid on delivered value, not on orders

Feed the platforms a conversion value that reflects what actually arrives and stays. If COD returns run at the rates Unicommerce reported, an order-optimised campaign is quietly buying you RTO. Passing net revenue (or a predicted delivered value) back through your conversion API changes what the algorithm chases. Our [performance marketing](/services/performance-marketing) work starts here because every other optimisation sits on top of it.

### Spread creative across the window

Growster's Harshit Arora put the gap between the brands that grew 50% and the ones that shrank 25% down to having a fresh, relevant ad for every peak day. Admerly's data that regional-language ads drew 38% more festive engagement than English ones points to where that freshness should come from. A Hindi, Tamil or Bengali cut of your hero ad is cheaper to make than another week of rising CPMs, and it speaks to the Tier 3 buyer who is now 41% of orders.

### Stop reading the average

The ClickPost spread (median brand at 19%, a third above 50%, a fifth down more than 25%) means the category average tells you almost nothing about your own business. Benchmark against your own last season, by channel and by tier. We covered the structure of that review in our piece on [D2C unit economics for 2026](/blog/d2c-unit-economics-2026).

## The honest caveat

The per-rupee figures are Growster's calculations built from other firms' reports, not a single audited dataset, and the datasets themselves use different windows and samples (Unicommerce covers all channels on its platform; ClickPost's cohort is 200 D2C brands). Treat the 19 to 24% as a well-reasoned estimate of the direction and size, then run the same division on your own CAC and AOV for the season. If your number came out higher than 24%, the basket is the first place to look, not the bid.
