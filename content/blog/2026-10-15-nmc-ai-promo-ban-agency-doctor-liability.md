On 6 October 2026 the National Medical Commission published its Guidelines on Ethical Advertising and Public Communication, effective immediately. The line that matters to anyone producing healthcare content: an AI-generated promotional campaign "for furtherance of commercial interest" is prohibited, and any AI content that does stay compliant must carry a source mark stating its origin is AI.

The second line matters more to agencies. Using an agency, influencer or platform does not shield the doctor or the hospital if they authorised, commissioned, sponsored or knowingly permitted the content.

## What the guidelines actually say about AI

The guidelines treat AI-generated and AI-assisted content as advertising whenever its content or distribution has a promotional character. That closes the usual escape route of calling something "an explainer" while it sits inside a lead funnel.

Three separate AI rules sit inside the document. First, promotional campaigns made with AI for commercial gain are barred outright. Second, AI content that remains permissible must compulsorily carry a source mark explicitly stating that the origin is AI. Third, AI cannot be used to create or alter a patient's image, testimonial, voice or clinical outcome, or to fabricate an endorsement.

Content built on patient data fed into AI tools also has to meet privacy, confidentiality, data-protection and consent rules. An exemption exists for AI campaigns tied to Union or state government health programmes serving a larger public interest. A private clinic's Diwali health-check offer is not that.

## The clause that changes agency contracts

Most clients we work with in healthcare assume liability sits with whoever pressed publish. Under these guidelines it sits with the registered practitioner or the institution that approved the work. The medical register is theirs, not ours.

The penalty ladder reported by MediaNama is graded, enforced by State Medical Councils, and each step requires a show-cause notice and a reasoned order first.

| Stage | Consequence |
|---|---|
| First violation | Warning and mandatory ethics training |
| Second | Censure and a monetary penalty |
| Third | Registration suspended for 3 to 6 months |
| Serious | Registration suspended for 6 to 12 months |
| Repeated | Removed from the register for 1 to 3 years |

Appeals go to the appropriate appellate authority within 60 days. There is no separate enforcement mechanism aimed at agencies or ad platforms, and MediaNama notes there is no dedicated system to detect AI medical ads or force takedowns. That gap is not comfort. It means the exposure lands on the one person with a licence to lose, and that person will go looking for who briefed the content.

> The penalty falls on the licence holder, so the brief is where the risk gets created or removed.

## What stops being viable

Some formats we see in healthcare feeds every week are now hard to defend.

An AI avatar of "Dr. X" explaining a procedure inside a paid campaign is an AI-generated promotion for commercial interest. An AI-voiced patient story, even a "dramatised" one, falls under the ban on creating or altering a patient's testimonial or voice. A before-and-after sequence enhanced or generated with AI is doubly out, since patient photos, videos and records cannot be used in advertising unless the law permits it and the consent and safeguards are in place.

Real patient material is not rescued by consent either. The guidelines say consent does not, by itself, make an otherwise prohibited practice permissible, and where consent is required it must be specific, informed, voluntary, documented and capable of verification.

Patient testimonials are a separate problem. On social media, doctors shall not request or share patient testimonials, and buying, manipulating or publishing fake or paid reviews and ratings is prohibited.

## What still works

Health-awareness, public-health, academic and educational content is allowed, provided it does not promote a personal practice, solicit patients or get monetised through promotional marketing. That is a narrow lane, but it is a real one, and it is where credible doctor-led content has always done its best work.

Disclosure is mandatory in electronic media posts: the doctor's name, qualifications, registration status and State or National Medical Register number. Clinical establishments must disclose the details of the doctors associated with their posts. If you built templates after the earlier conduct rules, check that the registration number line is a fixed element, not something an editor can drop.

Billboards are limited to factual information such as infrastructure and facilities.

## A working checklist for the next 30 days

We are running this on every healthcare client now. It takes an afternoon per account.

1. List every live asset that used AI at any stage: scripts, voice, avatars, image enhancement, thumbnails. Separate "AI helped an editor" from "AI generated what the viewer sees."
2. Pull anything that is AI-generated and promotional. Do not label and keep it up unless you are certain it falls outside the commercial-interest ban.
3. Remove any AI-touched patient voice, face, story or outcome. No label fixes this.
4. Rewrite agency and creator agreements so the doctor's written approval is a recorded step, and so the brief states that no AI-generated promotional content will be commissioned.
5. Fix the disclosure block: name, qualifications, registration status, register number, on every electronic post.

Be honest with clients about what is unsettled. The guidelines do not define where "promotional" ends for AI-assisted editing, and Medical Councils have not yet shown how they will treat a captioning tool or a background clean-up. Until they do, the safer reading is the one that protects the licence.
