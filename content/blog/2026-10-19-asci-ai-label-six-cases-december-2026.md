The Advertising Standards Council of India does not want a label on every ad that touched an AI tool. Its *Guidelines for Responsible Labelling of Synthetically Generated Content in Advertising*, issued on 29 September 2026, ask for a label only when the synthetic content "materially influences consumer decisions" and leaving it unlabelled could mislead. They take effect three months after publication, which puts the date at 29 December 2026.

That is narrower than most agency checklists we have seen. It is also stricter in a few places than people expect.

## The test is about the buyer, not the tool

The guidelines do not define "materially influences", and Khaitan & Co's note on them says the test is fact-sensitive and looks at consumer outcomes rather than the technology used. In practice the question we ask at creative sign-off is simple: if this viewer knew the frame was generated, would they value the product differently?

A cloud of AI-generated bokeh behind a skincare bottle: no. An AI-generated before-and-after on that same skin: yes, and that one may not be rescued by a label at all (more on that below).

## The six cases that need a label

ASCI lists the situations where disclosure is mandatory. Reading them against the briefs we get from clients, they reduce to six:

1. Synthetic influencers and brand ambassadors.
2. A real person's face or voice replicated, with consent, for personalised messaging.
3. Fabricated events or settings that change how the product is understood.
4. Demonstrations of products that do not exist yet.
5. Sound effects closely tied to a core product feature (ASCI's example is headphones).
6. Paid or sponsored AI-generated product suggestions, which must read "Sponsored by [Brand]".

The suggested wording is "Audio/Video created using AI" or "Audio/Video enhanced using AI". Platform-provided AI labels are accepted, and other wording works if it describes the synthetic content accurately.

Case four is the one D2C founders miss. If your launch film shows a product prototype rendered in a video model because the factory sample is not ready, that is a demonstration of a product that does not exist. It needs the label.

## What you can leave unlabelled

Exemptions are reasonably generous. Routine editing such as colour correction, lighting adjustment, noise reduction and minor blemish removal needs nothing. The same goes for decorative or ambient elements, obviously fantastical effects, and accessibility uses like subtitles and translations.

Text is exempt too. ASCI treats administrative and text-based use, such as generating ad copy, as outside the labelling duty. So an AI-assisted script on its own triggers nothing.

| Use of AI | Label needed? |
| --- | --- |
| Colour grade, noise reduction, blemish cleanup | No |
| AI-written ad copy or captions | No |
| Subtitles and translated audio descriptions | No |
| Decorative AI background, clearly fantastical effects | No |
| AI ambassador or synthetic influencer | Yes |
| Consented voice or face clone for personalised messages | Yes |
| Product demo for a product that does not exist yet | Yes |
| Sponsored AI product suggestion | Yes, "Sponsored by [Brand]" |

## What a label cannot fix

This is the part that matters most for performance creative. ASCI lists uses that are barred whether or not a label is attached: fabricated endorsements or testimonials, synthetic exaggeration of results or features, non-existent locations presented as real, unauthorised copyrighted work, and deepfakes or likeness used without consent.

> A label discloses the method. It does not make a misleading claim true.

Every synthetic ad is still assessed against the full ASCI Code. A weight-loss visual that no customer could achieve is a misleading-claims problem in January exactly as it is today. Adding "created using AI" to it changes nothing.

## Who carries it

The responsibility sits with the advertiser. Exchange4media's report on the release says plainly that it "remains with advertisers", and Khaitan's note adds that the document sets no separate duties for agencies or publishers. It asks advertisers to review synthetic content in their approval process, document why they labelled or did not, and coordinate disclosures with agencies and platform partners.

For us that means a written line in every creative brief: which of the six cases applies, and if none, why. Most clients we work with have never recorded that decision. After 29 December, "we assumed it did not need one" will not be a defence you can document.

## How this sits with the IT Rules

Do not confuse this with the government rules. The IT (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Amendment Rules, 2026, notified in February, put labelling duties on platforms for synthetically generated information under Rule 3(3). Those apply to intermediaries and to uploaders through platform declarations. ASCI's guidelines apply to advertisers and only where a consumer decision is influenced.

The practical result is two separate checks. A platform may add its own AI label because the upload is synthetic, and your ad can still breach ASCI because the synthetic part misled. Our earlier piece on [why a Veo watermark is not the label India wants](/blog/veo-watermark-vs-india-label) covers the platform side.

One enforcement caveat: ASCI decisions carry no statutory penalty. Non-compliance is a breach of the Code and typically ends in a request to modify or withdraw the ad. The harder consequences come from the other laws that sit behind several of the banned uses, including the CCPA's rules on misleading advertising and personality-rights injunctions.

## What to do before 29 December

Pull every AI-touched asset in your live and planned library and sort it against the six cases. Anything in case two, three or four gets the label wording above. Anything in the barred list gets redesigned, not labelled.

Then fix the process. If your video pipeline is moving towards generated footage, build the label into the edit template so it ships by default; that is cheaper than auditing later.
