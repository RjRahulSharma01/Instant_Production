An Indian online pharmacy can now buy Google Ads. It has to be certified by LegitScript's Healthcare Merchant Certification Program first, then separately approved by Google. And after all of that it still cannot name a prescription drug in an ad or on the landing page that ad points to, and it cannot bid on prescription drug keywords in India at all. The channel opened. The vocabulary did not.

Google posted the change on 7 April 2026 and LegitScript announced the expanded partnership the next day. India was added to the location specifics of Google's Healthcare and medicines policy under a single line item: online pharmacies, allowed with limitations.

## What the India entry actually says

Google's country table for India now carries four rows, and only one of them moved this year.

| Business type | Status in India | What it requires |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Online pharmacies | Allowed with limitations | LegitScript certification, then Google certification. No prescription drugs in ads or landing pages. |
| Pharmaceutical manufacturers | Allowed with limitations | Over-the-counter medicines only, in line with Indian law |
| Telemedicine providers | Not listed for India | Nothing to apply for yet |
| Pre-natal gender determination | Not allowed | Blocked outright, on a Supreme Court order |

That third row is the one people misread. The April announcement covered two countries at once, and the telemedicine half of it applied to New Zealand, not India. Indonesia has a telemedicine row in the same policy table. India does not. A consultation-first health platform in Bengaluru reading the headline and briefing its media team to prepare a certification application is preparing for a door that has not opened.

## The two limits that decide the media plan

The first limit is the one Google states directly on the India row: advertisers cannot promote prescription drugs in ads or landing pages. Not the ad copy, not the destination. For a pharmacy, the destination is a product catalogue, which is exactly where prescription molecules live.

The second limit is quieter, and it sits in a different policy article. Google's Prescription drug services policy carries two separate country lists. One covers where these services may be promoted at all. The other covers where approved advertisers may bid on keywords containing prescription drug terms. India appears on the first list. India does not appear on the second.

Australia, the UK, Canada, Germany, Japan, Indonesia, New Zealand and around twenty other markets sit on both. India sits on one. So an Indian pharmacy can advertise that it is a pharmacy, and cannot buy the search terms that most people actually type when they want medicine.

> The demand signal that converts best in this category is the one Google will not sell you in India.

## What is left to bid on

Quite a lot, once you stop looking for molecule names. The queries that survive are the ones about the service rather than the substance: home delivery of medicines in a named city, uploading a prescription, refill reminders, chronic care subscriptions, generic substitution, delivery timelines, price comparison on non-prescription categories. Over-the-counter, wellness, diagnostics booking and devices are separate policy surfaces and behave normally.

The practical consequence is that a category everyone assumed would be won on search intent is going to be won on brand demand and on the mid-funnel. That is a very different budget shape. Most clients we work with in regulated verticals arrive expecting search to carry 60 to 70 percent of the plan, and the honest answer here is that search is the smaller half.

If you are building the plan, the [performance marketing](/services/performance-marketing) question to settle first is not which keywords to buy. It is which non-prescription surfaces can carry enough volume to make certification worth the effort at all.

## The certification sequence, and the order matters

LegitScript first, Google second. LegitScript's review covers licensure, prescribing practices, patient privacy protections and advertising transparency, and it is a paid, evidence-based audit rather than a form. Google's own application then goes in at the child account level, never at the manager account level, with the customer ID and the certified domain attached. Agencies applying on a client's behalf have to send documentation of that relationship.

Two things are worth knowing before the finance conversation. Certification is per location, so a separate application is needed for every market you plan to advertise in. And enforcement on this policy is not instant: Google says violations will not trigger immediate suspension, and a warning arrives at least seven days before any account action. That window is a genuine safety margin for a catalogue site where a single new SKU page can move you out of compliance.

## Why Google is stricter than Indian law right now

There is still no notified rule in India governing e-pharmacy operations. The Drugs and Cosmetics (Amendment) Rules proposed in 2018 were never notified. The Delhi High Court directed the central government in November 2023 to frame a policy for online medicine sales, and the draft has remained under review since. In the meantime online pharmacies operate under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940 and its 1945 Rules, plus the Pharmacy Act, 1948: a valid prescription for Schedule H, H1 and X drugs, dispensing by a registered pharmacist.

So the strictest advertising gate an Indian online pharmacy faces today is not statutory. It is a private certification standard operated from Portland, Oregon, adopted by the platform where most of the category's demand shows up. That is an uncomfortable fact but a useful one, because it means the requirements are written down, dated and testable, which is more than the domestic position offers.

It also means the gate can move again. Google's ads policy change log lists 56 entries for 2026, including two separate healthcare and medicines updates in April and a third in July. Anyone treating this as settled should set a calendar reminder rather than a bookmark.

## What to do this month

If you run marketing for an Indian e-pharmacy, start the LegitScript application now and assume the timeline is measured in weeks, not days. While that runs, do the landing page audit, because it is the part that will actually fail: map every URL a campaign could send traffic to, and flag every page where a prescription drug name appears in a title, a breadcrumb, a filter label or a schema field. A category page called "Diabetes medicines" is a different risk from one called "Diabetes care."

If you run marketing for a telemedicine platform, do not start an application. Watch the policy article instead, and note that the New Zealand precedent suggests India's telemedicine row is plausible rather than imminent.

And if you are a hospital or clinic with no pharmacy arm, none of this changes your position. Your constraints remain the ones we covered in [safe content for doctors under NMC rules](/blog/nmc-safe-content-for-doctors), and the [healthcare](/industries/healthcare) advertising problem there is still a claims problem, not a certification one.

One caveat worth stating plainly: certification permits advertising. It does not validate a single claim you make. ASCI, the CCPA and the Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act, 1954 all still apply to the copy itself, and a LegitScript badge is no defence against any of them.
