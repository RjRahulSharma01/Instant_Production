If a paid creator gives technical advice about health, nutrition, investing or insurance, ASCI expects their qualification to be on screen at the start, next to the brand. A "#ad" tag alone does not cover it. The rule sits in the ASCI Guidelines for Influencer Advertising in Digital Media, and it applies only where there is a material connection between the creator and the brand.

Most briefs we see ask for the ad disclosure and say nothing about the credentials line. That is the gap.

## What the rule actually says

ASCI first announced the qualification requirement for health and finance influencers on 17 August 2023. Its CEO and Secretary General, Manisha Kapoor, said at the time that a "one size fits all" approach can be dangerous in these areas, and that consumers should follow only qualified experts when engaging with brands or products in them.

The scope was narrowed by Addendum II, dated 7 April 2025 and announced on 28 April 2025. Qualifications are now needed where the content gives technical information or specific advice. They are not needed where the advertisement is generic or a public service message.

So a lifestyle creator can say "get your annual health check-up done" in a branded post without a medical degree. The same creator cannot say which supplement dose helps with a thyroid problem, or which mutual fund to hold, without one.

## Which qualifications count

The guidelines name the credentials. For health and nutrition: a medical degree, or certification as a nurse, nutritionist, dietician, physiotherapist or psychologist. For finance: SEBI registration for stock and investment advice (with the registration number stated), an IRDAI insurance licence, or chartered accountant or company secretary status for other financial advice.

| Content type | Credential ASCI names | Where the line goes |
|---|---|---|
| Stock or investment advice | SEBI registration, number stated | Opening of the video, or before the text begins |
| Insurance product advice | IRDAI licence | Same |
| Tax, accounting, compliance advice | CA or CS | Same |
| Diet, supplement, nutrition advice | Nutritionist or dietician certification, or medical degree | Same |
| Exercise or injury advice | Physiotherapy qualification, or medical degree | Same |
| Mental wellbeing advice | Psychologist certification, or medical degree | Same |

## Where the credentials line goes

Placement is specific. For video, the qualification must be superimposed prominently and upfront, or stated as the opening remark. For text and blogs, it comes before the content begins. For podcasts and audio, it is called out at the beginning.

This is different from the ad tag. A tag can sit in the caption or a corner overlay. The credentials line has to arrive before the advice does. If the first ten seconds of a reel are the hook and the qualification only appears at second 40, we treat that as a miss.

> The credentials line is not decoration for the ad. It is the condition that makes the advice allowed to be in the ad at all.

## Why this is worth checking now

ASCI's Half-Yearly Complaints Report for 2025-26 examined 1,173 influencer advertisements and found that 98% needed modification. It also found that 76% of the country's top digital influencers breached the mandatory disclosure norms. That report does not break violations down by profession, so we cannot tell you how many were credential failures. What it does show is that reviewers are reading influencer content at volume, and health and finance claims are the easiest ones to flag on sight.

We cover the broader FY26 numbers in [our breakdown of ASCI's influencer violations](/blog/asci-influencer-violations-fy26). For finance specifically, the SEBI side of the same problem is in [our post on the SEBI ad code and celebrity finfluencers](/blog/sebi-ad-code-finfluencer-celebrity).

## Who carries the risk

Legal commentary on the 2025 addendum places the primary responsibility for having and showing the qualification on the influencer, and advises brands to verify credentials before a partnership starts. We would go further in practice. An ASCI complaint names the advertisement, and the advertiser is the party that has to defend it. Treat verification as the brand's job.

## What we put in briefs now

Three changes, all cheap.

First, a claim-level split in the brief. We mark each talking point as generic (awareness, check-ups, "talk to your doctor") or technical (doses, products, returns, tax treatment). Technical points go only to creators who hold a listed qualification.

Second, a proof step before contracting. For finance we ask for the SEBI registration number and check it against SEBI's public intermediary listing. For health we ask for the degree or registration certificate. For anyone who cannot supply one, the brief is cut down to generic content or the creator is swapped.

Third, a script line that carries the credentials: name, qualification, registration number where relevant, said in the first sentence and placed as an on-screen overlay in the first frames. We approve this line before the edit, not after, so it does not get trimmed in the cut.

The honest caveat: ASCI's guidelines are a self-regulatory code, and the line between "generic" and "technical" is a judgement call that ASCI's complaints panel makes case by case. If a script sits near that line, assume it will be read as technical and add the credentials line anyway. It costs one sentence.
