If your brand sets a custom commission rate in YouTube's new affiliate campaigns, every creator who tags that product is now in a material connection with you, whether or not you ever spoke to them. Under the ASCI influencer guidelines, that makes the video your advertisement, and you share responsibility for what it claims.

That is the part of yesterday's Made On YouTube announcements that Indian D2C brands should read twice. The headline for most people was that Amazon product tagging is coming to India "in the coming weeks". The bigger shift for brands is the second bullet under it.

## What YouTube actually announced on 23 September

In a post by Amjad Hanif and Travis Katz, YouTube said three things that matter here. The Shopping affiliate program will reach 35 countries by the end of the year. Amazon tagging, which launched in the US, is expanding to India and Brazil. And there are new "affiliate campaigns" that let retailers and brands offer exclusive commission rates or bonuses when creators feature specific products.

India has had the affiliate program since October 2024, when YouTube launched it with Flipkart and Myntra. YouTube's own help page now describes how the campaigns work. Merchants create custom commission rates that sit above the standard affiliate rate. Creators see them in the Shopping tab or their Studio inbox. The elevated rate applies when the creator tags an eligible product during the campaign window, and the page says plainly that the campaign "may apply automatically to eligible transactions if you tag the products".

So a brand can now raise what it pays per sale to a whole pool of creators at once, without a brief, a contract or a conversation.

## Why ASCI treats this as your ad

The ASCI Guidelines for Influencer Advertising in Digital Media define a material connection as "any connection between an advertiser and influencer that may affect the weight or credibility of the representation". The list that follows starts with monetary or other compensation. A commission you have chosen to raise is compensation you set, for content about your product.

Two clauses then do the work.

Clause 1.3 lists the labels ASCI accepts, and "Affiliate" is one of them, alongside "Ad", "Sponsored" and the "Includes Paid Promotion" tag on YouTube. ASCI already expects affiliate content to be labelled. That part is not new.

Clause 1.5 is the one brands tend to skip. Where an advertiser has a material connection with an influencer, the advertiser is responsible for making sure the post complies with the ASCI Code, and should call on the influencer to edit or delete it when needed. The influencer carries the disclosure duty; the brand carries the content.

> A standard affiliate rate is the retailer paying a creator. A custom rate is you paying them, and the ASCI Code follows the money.

The Department of Consumer Affairs made the same point in its January 2023 "Endorsement Know-hows!" guide under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019: anyone who can influence purchase decisions must disclose any material connection, and the list includes monetary compensation. That guide sits on top of the CCPA's 2022 guidelines on misleading advertisements, which apply to the manufacturer as well as the endorser.

## The three situations, side by side

Not every tagged video pulls the brand in. The difference is who is paying and whether you raised it.

| Situation | Who pays the creator | Material connection with the brand | Brand shares content responsibility |
|---|---|---|---|
| Creator bought the product and tags it at the retailer's standard rate | The retailer (Flipkart, Myntra, Amazon) | Arguably not with the brand | Weak case |
| Brand runs a YouTube affiliate campaign with a custom rate | The brand, via the elevated commission | Yes | Yes, under ASCI clause 1.5 |
| Brand pays a flat fee and the creator also tags the product | The brand, twice | Yes | Yes |

The first row still needs the creator to disclose the affiliate relationship with the retailer. It just does not make the claims in the video your problem. The second row does, and it is the row that did not exist at scale a week ago.

## The "unbriefed creator" problem

With a normal influencer campaign, we write a brief, share the claims the brand can substantiate, and review the cut before it goes live. An affiliate campaign skips all of that. A creator you have never heard of can see a 4% bump on your serum, make a Short calling it "dermatologist-approved", tag it, and collect the elevated rate.

That video is now connected to you. If your brand cannot substantiate "dermatologist-approved", you are the advertiser of an unsubstantiated claim you never wrote. ASCI's second addendum adds another layer in health and finance: influencers giving technical advice in those categories must hold and state qualifications. A skincare campaign that pulls in a creator making treatment claims walks straight into that.

We see this pattern already on Instagram with seeded products. Affiliate campaigns remove even the seeding step, because the incentive is visible to anyone eligible in the Shopping hub.

## What the commission actually has to beat

Before running a campaign, look at what creators already earn without you. Amazon's Associates advertising fee schedule for India, updated August 2026, pays 10% on apparel, shoes, watches and beauty, 5% on kitchen and home, 4.7% on grocery and health and personal care, and 3.5% on electronics. Many phone models earn 0%.

YouTube has not yet published the India rates for Amazon tags, and they may not mirror the Associates schedule, so treat those numbers as a benchmark rather than a promise. The practical point holds. In beauty, a creator can already earn a double-digit commission by tagging a competitor. A bonus that moves them to your product needs to be meaningful, and the more meaningful it is, the more clearly it is compensation.

YouTube also says commissions are paid through AdSense 60 to 120 days after purchase and reversed on returns. Creators will weigh that delay against a flat fee, which is why we expect many brands to combine the two.

## What we would set up before switching a campaign on

**Limit eligible products to claims you can defend.** If a product's efficacy claims are still being substantiated, keep it out of the campaign. The campaign detail page shows creators specific products and rates; that list is your first control.

**Publish a claims sheet creators can find.** You cannot brief every creator, but you can keep a public page of approved claims and required disclosures and point to it from your affiliate listing and outreach. It gives you something to cite when you ask for an edit.

**Monitor tagged videos every week.** YouTube's affiliate metrics show which videos drive campaign sales. Review those videos for disclosure (the paid promotion box plus an on-screen or spoken "Affiliate" or "Ad" label) and for claims. ASCI's own duration rules apply: for a video between 15 seconds and 2 minutes, the label should stay on screen for a third of its length.

**Write the takedown step down.** Clause 1.5 expects you to ask for edits or deletions. Decide in advance who sends that message and how fast, because a campaign with a hundred creators will produce at least one problem video.

**Keep flat-fee and affiliate deals in one tracker.** Many of the brands we work with run [influencer marketing](/services/influencer-marketing) through one team and marketplace affiliates through another. Under this setup, they are the same compliance exposure and need one owner.

## The honest caveat

ASCI has not yet ruled on a complaint involving a YouTube affiliate campaign specifically, and YouTube's Amazon tagging is not live in India at the time of writing. The reasoning above comes from applying the existing material connection definition and clause 1.5 to a new payment mechanism. It is the conservative reading, and it is the one we are giving clients.
