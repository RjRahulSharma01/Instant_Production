When YouTube dubs your English product video into Hindi, the voiceover changes language and the on-screen disclaimer does not. ASCI's disclaimer guidelines say a disclaimer must match the language of the ad's claims. So the dubbed version can carry a Hindi claim with an English caveat, and that is a gap you should close before the dub goes live.

## What YouTube actually shipped

On 4 February 2026, YouTube announced that auto dubbing is available to everyone in 27 languages. Eligible creators switch it on in YouTube Studio. A separate Expressive Speech layer, which tries to keep the original tone and energy, covers 8 languages: English, French, German, Hindi, Indonesian, Italian, Portuguese and Spanish. Lip sync, which matches mouth movements to the translated audio, is only a pilot.

Viewers also got a Preferred Language setting. Before this, the audio language defaulted from watch history. Now a viewer can pin Hindi, and YouTube will serve the Hindi track when one exists. YouTube said that in December it averaged more than 6 million daily viewers who watched at least 10 minutes of auto dubbed content.

Two details matter for brands. YouTube says auto dubs have no negative impact on the original video's discovery. And creators can upload their own dub or turn auto dubbing off for a video. The announcement does not say how dubbed videos are labelled, so we are not going to assume a label does any compliance work for you.

## Where the compliance problem sits

The ASCI Code, Chapter I, says advertisements shall neither distort facts nor mislead the consumer by implication or omission. A claim and its qualifier are read together. If the qualifier is missing or unreadable for the person hearing the claim, the omission is the problem.

ASCI's disclaimer guidelines add the practical rules. As reported when they were tightened, a disclaimer must be in the same language as the claims in the ad, and in a bilingual ad it must follow the dominant language. Each disclaimer line has to stay on screen for more than four seconds, with a limit of two full-length lines and one disclaimer per frame. ASCI's own survey of 130 consumers found 80 did not notice the disclaimer at all.

Put those together with auto dubbing. The audio now speaks to a Hindi viewer. The burned-in caption underneath still speaks to an English one. For that viewer, the disclaimer is in the wrong language, which is the exact case ASCI wrote the language rule for.

One honest caveat: YouTube describes auto dubbing for videos on a channel, not for paid ad units. But a brand video promoting a product is advertising under the ASCI Code whether it runs as a paid ad or sits on the brand's channel, and that is how we treat it with clients. Where a creator channel publishes a brand integration, the same logic applies to the brand.

## What changes in a dubbed video

| Element | Original English video | Auto-dubbed Hindi version | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spoken claim | English | Hindi, machine-translated | Wording drifts from your approved claim |
| Burned-in disclaimer | English | Still English | Language mismatch with the claim |
| Subtitles | English | Not regenerated for the dub | Viewer reads one language, hears another |
| Hold time | Checked at four seconds per line | Hindi reads slower in some lines | Disclaimer may fall below the hold time |

The first row is the quiet one. A machine translation of "up to 40% off on select styles" can lose "up to" or "select". If the dub drops the qualifier, the spoken claim is stronger than the one you cleared, and no on-screen text fixes that.

## A workflow that holds up

We would not turn the feature off across the board. Reach in regional languages is real, and our post on [building a Hindi master for YouTube auto-dubbing](/blog/hindi-master-youtube-auto-dubbing) covers why a Hindi-first source often beats a dub. For anything that carries a claim, run this order.

First, list every claim and qualifier in the video, spoken and on screen. Second, decide per video whether auto dubbing is on or off. Anything with a price, a health or finance claim, a guarantee or a comparison should be off until a human has cleared the dub. YouTube gives creators that switch and also lets them upload their own audio track.

Third, if you upload your own dub, get the translation checked against the approved claim wording, line by line. Fourth, rebuild the disclaimer in the dub's language and re-time it, so each line holds past four seconds at a Hindi reading pace. The same timing logic is in our note on [disclaimer duration and placement](/blog/ad-tag-duration-placement-asci).

Fifth, keep a short file with the original claim, the dubbed claim, the translation sign-off and a screenshot of the localised disclaimer. If ASCI asks for substantiation, the Code says the advertiser must produce it, and a dated file is quicker than reconstructing it.

> A dub is a second ad that happens to share a thumbnail with the first.

## Why Hindi is the sharp edge

Hindi is the one Indian language named in the Expressive Speech list, so it is where dubs will sound most natural and be least likely to get a second listen from your team. A convincing voice makes a wrong claim more persuasive, not less. Most clients we work with check the English cut three times and the dub zero times, because the dub arrives after sign-off. Move the dub check inside the approval round, and give the Hindi reviewer the same claim sheet the English reviewer had.

## The practical recommendation

Treat each auto-dubbed language as its own deliverable with its own approval, not as a free setting. For a catalogue of simple brand stories with no claims, leave auto dubbing on and spot-check the Hindi. For anything with an offer or a regulated claim, switch it off, commission the localised audio and disclaimer through your [video production](/services/video-production) process, and publish only what has been read in the language it will be heard in.
