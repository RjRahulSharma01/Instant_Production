If your AI video pipeline can produce a recognisable human face or a recognisable human voice, the release form now matters more than the model. Between February and July 2026, the Delhi and Bombay High Courts issued at least eight interim orders restraining AI-generated likenesses. India still has no statute on personality rights. The courts did not wait for one.

## The eight orders

Every one of these is an interim order — granted fast, often ex parte, mostly against unnamed John Doe defendants plus the platforms carrying the content.

| Order | Court | Month | What was restrained |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Swami Ramdev | Delhi HC | Feb 2026 | AI deepfakes misappropriating persona |
| Jubin Nautiyal | Delhi HC | Feb 2026 | Synthesised singing voice, AI avatars |
| Sonakshi Sinha | Delhi HC | Mar 2026 | AI-generated deepfakes |
| Dr Sanjiv Goenka, CS(COMM) 395/2026 | Delhi HC | Apr 2026 | Generative AI, face morphing, superimposition, AI chatbots |
| Allu Arjun, CS(COMM) 403/2026 | Delhi HC | Apr 2026 | Voice cloning, gestures, dialogues, metaverse use |
| Naga Chaitanya | Delhi HC | Jun 2026 | Deepfakes, unauthorised merchandise |
| Preity Zinta | Bombay HC | Jun 2026 | AI and digital misuse of persona |
| Ravindra Shukla (Ravi Kishan), 2026 SCC OnLine Del 4973 | Delhi HC | Jul 2026 | Name, image, voice, catchphrases via AI, generative AI, machine learning, deepfakes |

The Ravi Kishan order, decided on 2 July 2026 by Justice Jyoti Singh, is the one worth reading in full if you only read one. It carries a takedown clock: website operators and domain registrars had three days to remove the listed URLs, and if they did not, Meta, Google and X were directed to act within 72 hours of being notified. That is a mechanism, not a sentiment. It means an aggrieved individual can get your creative off a platform inside a week without ever proving damages.

## What the courts decided a persona includes

The Allu Arjun order is the most instructive for anyone briefing an AI video shoot, because the court enumerated what it was protecting. Not just the name and the face. It listed iconic dialogues, the "Thaggede Le" hand movement, dance style, mannerisms and vocal delivery — and noted he had registered trademarks over his name and associated marks under the Trade Marks Act, 1999.

So the workaround most teams reach for first does not work. Generating a face that is *nearly* the celebrity, or a voice that is *evocative of* the celebrity, or a gesture the audience will read as the celebrity, is the thing being restrained. The Delhi High Court in the Goenka matter listed the technologies by name in its injunction: artificial intelligence, generative AI, machine learning, deepfakes, AI chatbots, face morphing, superimposition.

The court in that same matter also drew a line on parody. Caricature and lampooning stay protected speech. Content that is defamatory, misleading or commercially exploitative does not get to shelter under artistic expression. For an advertiser this distinction is close to useless in practice — an ad is commercial by definition, so the parody defence was never available to you.

## Where the brand's own liability sits

Most of these suits target impersonators, scam operators and porn sites, not brands. That has made a lot of marketing teams read the coverage and file it under someone else's problem. It is not.

ASCI's draft AI disclosure guidelines, released for consultation until 13 June 2026, place "using a person's likeness or voice without consent" in the high-risk tier — the tier where an AI label does not cure the violation. The content has to be withdrawn or restructured. Fabricated endorsements, fabricated testimonials and AI-generated fake authority figures (the invented doctor recommending a supplement) sit in the same bucket.

The consent case is the one people get wrong. Get a signed release and you move out of the prohibited tier — but replicating a real person's likeness or voice with AI lands in medium risk, which carries a mandatory, prominent disclosure. Consent changes which rule applies. It does not remove the rule.

> Consent is what keeps you out of court. It is not what keeps you out of the label.

We flag this on every brief that involves a founder cameo or a customer testimonial rebuilt with AI. Clients assume the signature on the release closes the file. It closes exactly one of two files. The other one is the on-screen disclosure, and that ties back to [the labelling rules that came into force in February](/blog/ai-video-ad-labelling-india).

## What we changed in our own process

Three things, all boring, all cheap compared to a takedown notice.

**Faces are sourced, not summoned.** Any human face in an AI-generated frame traces back to either a licensed model with an AI-usage clause in the release, or a fully synthetic character we can document from prompt to render. There is no third category. If someone on the team cannot say which of the two a face is, it does not ship.

**Voice gets the same paperwork as face.** This is the gap in most agencies. Voice cloning shows up in three of the eight orders above, and Jubin Nautiyal's suit was specifically about a synthesised singing voice. Teams that would never use an actor's face without a release will happily clone a voice from a founder's old webinar audio, because it does not feel like a likeness. Legally it is.

**We keep the prompt log.** For every AI-generated human in a campaign we retain the prompts, the reference images fed in, and the model and version used. If a claim arrives, the question will be whether you targeted a real person's identity. A prompt log that shows a synthetic character built from scratch answers that in an afternoon. Reconstructing it six months later from memory does not.

None of this slows a shoot down meaningfully. It adds a column to the asset tracker. The [video production](/services/video-production) work we do with AI in the pipeline now assumes this column exists from the brief stage rather than the delivery stage, because retrofitting provenance onto a finished cut is where the cost actually is.

## The honest caveat

These are interim orders, not final judgments. Several were granted ex parte, meaning the court heard one side. None of them has been tested on appeal yet, and an interim injunction is not a statement of settled law. Someone reading this in 2027 may find the scope has narrowed.

But the direction is not ambiguous, and the practical exposure does not depend on the law settling. A John Doe order plus a 72-hour platform takedown obligation means a campaign can be pulled off Meta and YouTube while the legal question is still open. For a brand mid-flight on a launch, that is the damage — not the eventual ruling.

## What to do this week

Pull every campaign asset from the last six months that contains a human face or voice you did not film or record yourself. For each one, answer two questions: can you name the person who consented, and can you produce the document? If either answer is no, that asset is in ASCI's high-risk tier today, regardless of what label sits on it.

Then fix the intake, not the archive. The cheapest version of this problem is a required field in your creative brief template. The most expensive version is a 72-hour takedown during a launch week.
