If your ad shows a topper, the topper's rank is the smallest part of what you owe the viewer. The Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA) has now fined two coaching institutes for exactly this: showing successful candidates without saying which course they actually took, and without written consent obtained after the result.

The orders came on 15 May 2026. Motion Education Pvt. Ltd. was fined ₹10 lakh and Career Line Coaching (CLC), Sikar, ₹5 lakh. Both were pulled up under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, for misleading advertisement (Section 2(28)) and unfair trade practice (Section 2(47)).

## What CCPA actually found

The headline numbers in the ads were big. Motion's creatives claimed, for the 2025 exam cycle, a 91.2% NEET success rate (6,972 of 7,645 students) and a 51.02% JEE Advanced rate (3,231 of 6,332). CLC claimed 1,650+ selections across MBBS, IIT and other courses.

The problem was not the arithmetic. CCPA's finding was that the students shown had often joined only a free online batch, a scholarship programme (Motion's free "I-Eklavya" course) or a test series, while the ad sold the paid classroom programme. Some students, per the reporting on the orders, were enrolled only after the exam had concluded. And the institutes could not produce proper written consent for using the students' photographs and names.

CCPA also gave the institutes repeated opportunities to back the success-rate claims with documents. They did not. That detail matters more than people think: a notice is not a surprise attack. It is a window to show your working, and both institutes let it close.

## The rule these orders enforce

The orders apply the Guidelines for Prevention of Misleading Advertisement in Coaching Sector, 2024, notified by CCPA on 13 November 2024. Three requirements in those guidelines map directly onto a ranker creative.

First, the rank, the name and the duration of the course must be shown alongside the candidate's photograph, and displayed prominently. A footnote in 6-point grey type is not prominent.

Second, the ad must make clear whether the student paid for the course. A free batch or a scholarship seat is a different product from the one on your pricing page.

Third, the student's name, photograph, testimonial or video can be used only with written consent obtained after selection. Consent collected at admission, in a terms-and-conditions tick box, does not meet that test.

The guidelines also list false claims about success rates and "guaranteed selection" as misleading in themselves.

## Where this breaks in a real creative workflow

We see the same failure pattern whenever we audit an education account. A result lands, the social team pulls the topper's photo from a WhatsApp group, the designer drops it into the template, and the performance team runs it across Meta and YouTube by evening. At no point does anyone ask what the student enrolled in.

The fix is a data problem before it is a creative problem. You need a record per ranker, kept somewhere the ad team can see it.

| Field to hold per ranker | Why a regulator would ask |
|---|---|
| Course enrolled in, and its duration | Guideline requires it beside the photo |
| Paid, free or scholarship | Must be stated if the course was not paid for |
| Enrolment date vs exam date | Post-exam enrolment looks like borrowed credit |
| Written consent, dated after the result | Consent before selection does not count |
| Where each creative using them is live | You will have to withdraw it all if consent is pulled |

> A topper is evidence of one specific course, not of your brand. The ad has to say which one.

## Why the free-batch point is the one that catches people

Most education brands run a scholarship test or a free demo batch as a top-of-funnel lever, and it works. The trouble starts when a student who topped out after a free test series becomes a poster for the paid course. From the student's side nothing is dishonest, since they did study with you. From a parent's side, the ad suggests that paying for the classroom programme is what produced the rank. That gap between what the student bought and what the viewer assumes they bought is the whole case.

## What to change this week

Pull every live creative that carries a student's face and match each one to the table above. Any ranker with a free batch, test-series or scholarship history needs that stated on the creative, or the creative comes down.

Then fix consent. If your consent form was signed at admission, send a fresh post-result consent request and pause the creative until it is signed. A WhatsApp "yes" is weak; a signed form with a date, the specific uses and the platforms listed is the version you want to be holding when a notice arrives.

Do the same for success-rate claims. A percentage like "91%" needs a numerator, a denominator and a definition of who is in each, held in a file you could hand over within days. If you cannot reconstruct it, do not publish it.

Finally, check the cost of getting this wrong against the cost of the work. CCPA has issued 60+ notices to coaching centres and imposed more than ₹1.39 crore in penalties across 31 institutes. The fines so far are modest next to a year of ad spend, but a withdrawal direction takes your best-performing creatives down overnight, and that is the part that hurts a performance account.
