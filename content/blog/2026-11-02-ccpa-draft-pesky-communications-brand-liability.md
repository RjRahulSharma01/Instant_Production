The Central Consumer Protection Authority's draft "Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of Pesky, Promotional and Unsolicited Commercial Communications, 2026" does one thing that matters more than the rest: it makes the brand liable, not only the agency or vendor that pressed send. If your name is in the message, or you benefit from it, the draft treats you as a party.

That changes who signs off on every WhatsApp broadcast, RCS push and lead follow-up call script.

## What the draft actually covers

The draft is issued under Section 18(2) of the Consumer Protection Act, 2019. It covers commercial communications in text, voice, image, video or file form, sent over telecom networks and internet services, including OTT apps, RCS and social media. Breaches can be pursued under Sections 20 and 21 of the Act, the same sections used for misleading advertisements and unfair trade practices.

Until now, most of the rulebook for promotional messaging sat with TRAI under the TCCCPR, 2018, which only touches telecom channels. This draft reaches the channels where most Indian D2C and fintech brands now do their follow-ups.

Transactional messages sent through registered channels, such as OTPs, banking alerts and delivery updates, are excluded. So are some government messages, communications under an existing contract, and professional-client communications.

## The three parties it names

According to the [Khaitan & Co. note on the draft](https://www.khaitanco.com/sites/default/files/2026-10/Ergo_CCPA%20Guidelines_6%20October%202026.pdf), the guidelines apply to the sender, the intended beneficiary of the communication, and anyone named in it. A brand can therefore be caught as beneficiary even when a third-party tool or agency sent the message.

> If an agency broadcasts to a list you cannot show consent for, the draft does not care whose login it was. It cares whose product was promoted.

We already see this gap in client accounts. The consent record lives in one vendor's export, the broadcast runs from another's dashboard, and nobody at the brand can produce both on request.

## The consent test is per brand and per product

The draft asks for explicit consent for each brand, beneficiary and product. It has to be recorded in verifiable digital form, and it has to be freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous.

For calls and texts over telecom, consent must come through the telecom provider's Digital Consent Acquisition facility. For WhatsApp, Telegram, RCS and social platforms, the CCPA says it will prescribe a verifiable mechanism. That mechanism is not published yet.

The draft also bars adding people to promotional groups or broadcast lists without explicit consent. A lead who filled a form for a home loan has not agreed to receive your credit card offers.

| Requirement | What the draft proposes | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Consent | Explicit, per brand and per product, verifiable | Mechanism for non-telecom channels to be prescribed |
| Opt-out | Free and simple, with confirmation | Proposed |
| Cooling-off after opt-out | 45, 60 or 90 days, no fresh consent request | Length undecided |
| Complaint handling | Acknowledge in 24 hours, resolve in 7 days | Shorter TCCCPR timelines prevail |
| Record retention | 180 or 365 days for consent, opt-outs, sender identity | Undecided |

## The cooling-off period is the line to watch

After a customer opts out, neither the brand nor its agent could ask for fresh consent for 45, 60 or 90 days. Today, a "we miss you" flow that re-asks for permission three days after an opt-out is common. Under this draft that flow is a violation waiting to happen.

Win-back sequences need a suppression list that is shared across every vendor, with a date on each entry. If your CRM, your WhatsApp provider and your dialer each keep their own unsubscribes, the cooling-off clock will be tracked three different ways.

## AI-generated voices and text need disclosure up front

Communications using AI-generated voice or text must disclose that at the outset, audibly on calls and visibly in text. Both the sender and the beneficiary brand would be liable.

## What the small-business pushback tells you

FISME's Secretary General Anil Bhardwaj has called the requirements "fundamentally disproportionate" for micro and small enterprises and asked for a risk-based approach, as reported by [MediaNama](https://www.medianama.com/2026/09/223-ccpa-draft-guidelines-spam-ai-promotions/). Expect the final text to soften somewhere. Do not assume it softens on brand liability.

Two other points are worth noting. The draft does not specify penalty amounts. And the Khaitan note observes that an earlier draft's provision for suspending sender identities appears to have been removed, so enforcement would run through CCPA orders after a hearing, including penalties and recalls.

## What to do this month

The draft is not law yet, and the cooling-off and retention periods are open. Waiting for the final text is reasonable for the numbers. It is not reasonable for the groundwork.

1. Ask every vendor that messages your customers to send you its consent log for one campaign. If it cannot, you have found your problem.
2. Merge opt-outs into a single suppression list with dates.
3. Remove any contact from a WhatsApp group or broadcast list that you cannot trace to a consent for that brand and product.
4. Check that your lead data was collected in line with the DPDP Act, as covered in [our consent-first data note](/blog/dpdp-consent-first-party-data).
5. Draft the AI disclosure line for voice and text now, and test it on a live call.
