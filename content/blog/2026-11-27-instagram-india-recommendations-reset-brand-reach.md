On 16 November 2026 Meta said Instagram in India now checks content against its Recommendations Guidelines before showing it to people who do not follow you, using AI models tuned specifically for India. Nothing is being banned. What changes is which brand posts can travel beyond your followers, and that is where most new customers find you.

For a founder or marketing head, the useful question is not whether Instagram got stricter. It is whether your account is still eligible to be recommended at all, and Instagram lets you check that yourself.

## What Meta actually changed

Meta's newsroom post, [An Update on the Content We Recommend on Instagram in India](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/11/an-update-on-the-content-we-recommend-on-instagram-in-india/), lists two changes. The first is better detection: systems now assess content directly against the Recommendations Guidelines before recommending it, instead of waiting for someone to report or hide it. The second is that those systems were improved specifically for India, to keep more sexually suggestive content and content depicting violence out of recommendations.

The surfaces covered are the ones that bring you strangers: Explore, Reels and recommended posts in Feed. Meta says people in India now report recommended content about half as often as they did a year ago. It gives no baseline or sample size, so treat that as Meta describing its own direction, not as a measured result.

The timing matters too. On 10 October 2026 the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) issued an advisory asking Meta to review its ranking and recommendation systems on Instagram and Facebook, so they do not unduly promote misleading, manipulated or out-of-context content, according to [The Pioneer](https://dailypioneer.com/india/meta-details-instagram-recommendation-changes-in-india-following-government-review-request). Meta's post does not link the two and cites user feedback instead. [Outlook India](https://www.outlookindia.com/national/instagram-content-algorithm-changes-new-meta-rules-recommendations-india) notes Reuters reported Meta had not clarified the link. Either way, the direction of travel in India is more screening before recommendation, not less.

## Two rulebooks, and brands usually read only one

Instagram runs two separate sets of rules, and most brand teams only think about the first.

| Rulebook | What it decides | What happens if you break it |
|---|---|---|
| Community Guidelines | Whether a post can stay up at all | The post is removed |
| Recommendations Guidelines | Whether a post can be shown to non-followers | The post stays up for followers but is not recommended |

The second rulebook is the quiet one. A post that falls foul of it does not disappear, nobody sends you a takedown notice, and your existing followers still see it. It simply stops reaching anyone new. On an account where growth depends on Reels being picked up by Explore, that looks exactly like "the algorithm stopped liking us".

Instagram's own guide for creators, [What Creators Need To Know About Instagram Recommendations](https://creators.instagram.com/blog/instagram-recommendations-eligibility-tips-creators), gives examples of content that can stay up but is not recommended: content that may depict violence, such as people fighting; content promoting regulated products, such as tobacco or vaping; and content that may be sexually suggestive, such as pictures of people in see-through clothing.

## Why this is an account problem, not a post problem

The part brand teams miss is escalation. According to the same Instagram guide, if you repeatedly post content that goes against the Recommendations Guidelines, or your profile photo or bio does, your entire account may become ineligible for recommendation, and none of your content will be recommended for a period of time. Recently or repeatedly having posts removed under the Community Guidelines can do the same.

> One borderline Reel costs you that Reel's reach. A pattern of them costs you the whole account's reach, including the posts that were fine.

That changes the risk for categories that sit close to the line by nature. A lingerie or swimwear label, a beauty brand shooting skin and body care, a fitness or wellness brand, a self-defence or martial arts academy, a bar or restaurant promoting drinks: none of this content is unlawful, and most of it will stay up. But if Meta's India-tuned models now read more of it as suggestive or violent before recommending it, the cost lands on non-follower reach first, and repeated hits can spread it across the account.

Brands in regulated categories should read this alongside the rules they already follow. Healthcare, fintech, education and real estate brands are used to checking claims against ASCI, NMC, SEBI or RERA before a post goes live. The Recommendations Guidelines are a platform rule rather than a law, but the habit is the same: decide whether something is fit to promote before you publish, not after the reach drops.

## Check eligibility before you blame the algorithm

Professional accounts can see whether their content is eligible to be recommended in Account Status. Instagram's creator guide gives the route as Settings, then Account, then Account Status; menus get reshuffled, so if it has moved, search Settings for "Account Status". Instagram's help page on [Account Status](https://help.instagram.com/539126347315373) describes it as the place to see removed content and how that affects your account.

What to do this week:

1. **Open Account Status on every brand handle you run.** It shows whether your content can currently be recommended across Explore, Reels and Feed recommendations, and gives sample posts or profile elements that may be causing problems.
2. **Fix or appeal what it flags.** You can edit or delete the posts it names. If you think Instagram got it wrong, the guide says you may be able to request another review.
3. **Check the profile as well as the grid.** Profile photo and bio count towards eligibility. A bio or profile image that leans suggestive can affect every post on the account.
4. **Review the last 30 days of Reels in borderline categories.** Look for anything a model could read as suggestive (sheer fabric, close body shots, bathing scenes) or violent (fight choreography, prank slaps, stunt falls). Decide in advance which of these are worth posting for followers only.
5. **Keep public and professional.** Instagram only recommends content from public accounts. Business and Creator accounts are public by default, but check any regional or campaign handles that were set up quickly.

While you are in the creator guide, its other eligibility basics still apply and get ignored often: avoid reuploading Reels already on Instagram or posting aggregated content, avoid watermarked or low-quality Reels, keep Reels under three minutes if you want them recommended, and put search keywords in the caption rather than the comments.

## What this means for planning

For most Indian brands this reset will be invisible, and that is the point: if your content is clearly fine, nothing changes. The brands that will feel it are the ones whose best-performing organic content sat near the edge, and the ones that never looked at Account Status and assumed falling reach was about posting frequency or hooks.

Two practical shifts follow. First, brief borderline categories with two versions of the same idea, one built to be recommended and one for followers or paid. Second, when reach drops, check eligibility before you change strategy. Rewriting a content calendar to fix what is really an account-level eligibility flag wastes a month.

If reach fell sharply in the last fortnight, open Account Status before you touch the calendar. Meta has published no India-specific reach data for brands, so the only reliable evidence is your own: compare non-follower reach in Insights for Reels posted before and after 16 November, category by category.
