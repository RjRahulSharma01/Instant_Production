A loan app that appears in RBI's public directory of digital lending apps has not been approved, authorised or endorsed by RBI. The Reserve Bank's own Digital Lending Directions, 2025 say so in terms, and they also require the lender to make sure that listing is "not misrepresented" in any marketing material. If your ad says "RBI approved app", it is the lender who carries the problem, and the agency that wrote the line will be the first call.

## What the directory is, and what it is not

The Reserve Bank of India (Digital Lending) Directions, 2025 were issued on 8 May 2025 under circular RBI/2025-26/36. Alongside them, RBI's press release 2025-2026/288 announced a public directory of digital lending apps (DLAs), found under Citizen's Corner on the RBI website as "DLA's deployed by Regulated Entities".

Regulated entities (banks and NBFCs) upload the details of every DLA they run or join through RBI's CIMS portal. The directory exists, in RBI's words, "for the limited purpose of aiding the customers in verifying the claim" that an app is associated with a regulated lender. That is a lookup tool for borrowers. It is not a licence.

Two sentences in the Directions matter more than any others for anyone writing creative:

- Para 17(v): RBI will not verify or validate the data submitted on CIMS. The list is only as accurate as what the lender typed in.
- Para 17(vi): inclusion of a DLA in the directory is not registration, authorisation or endorsement by RBI, and the regulated entity must ensure this is not misrepresented in marketing, promotional or other materials.

## The claims that are now risky

Most loan-app ad copy that goes wrong does so in small, plausible-sounding phrases. These are the ones we flag in review.

| Line in the ad | Why it is a problem |
|---|---|
| "RBI approved app" | RBI does not approve apps. Listing is not approval (para 17(vi)). |
| "Verified by RBI" | RBI does not validate CIMS data (para 17(v)). |
| "RBI registered lending app" | The registration RBI grants is to the NBFC or bank, not the app. |
| "Listed on RBI's official DLA directory" | Literally true, but used as a trust badge it implies endorsement. |
| "Powered by [NBFC name], RBI regulated" | Accurate if the NBFC is real and the app actually lends on its balance sheet. Say which. |

The distinction we draw with clients is between a statement of fact and a statement of status. "Loans are offered by XYZ Finance Ltd, an NBFC regulated by the Reserve Bank of India" is a fact the borrower can check. "RBI approved" is a status RBI has never conferred on an app.

## Why this lands on the lender, and then on you

Para 5(vii) of the Directions says the regulated entity "shall remain fully responsible and liable for all acts and omissions" of its lending service provider. Para 17(vi) puts the misrepresentation duty on the regulated entity directly. Performance creative produced by an agency or an affiliate network sits inside that chain.

In practice this changes who needs to sign off. We have seen fintech teams treat compliance review as something that happens at the landing page and the KFS screen. Ad copy, app store listing text, push notifications and creator scripts all count as "marketing, promotional or other materials". A paid social ad is the first thing a borrower sees, and it is the cheapest place for a regulator, a competitor or a journalist to find a bad claim.

Affiliates are the usual weak point. A performance partner who has never read the Directions will happily write "RBI listed, 100% safe" because it converts. The lender is still liable for it.

## What the borrower can legitimately be told

Trust claims are not banned. They need to be the true ones, and there are several that hold up.

First, name the lender. A named NBFC or bank is a fact the borrower can check, and it is a stronger trust signal than a vague badge anyway.

Second, point to the directory as a check the borrower can run, not as a credential you hold. "You can verify our lending partner on RBI's list of digital lending apps" invites scrutiny and does not claim endorsement.

Third, lead with the borrower protections that are actually in the Directions: a Key Fact Statement before the loan, an explicit exit option during the cooling-off period (para 10, set by the lender's board and not shorter than one day), and disbursal straight into the borrower's own bank account. These are specific and checkable, and unlike a seal they do not collapse when someone reads the circular.

## What this does to creative testing

Trust badges are one of the first things performance teams test, because "approved" and "verified" framing tends to lift click-through on cold traffic. We would not run that test with RBI language at all. A variant that wins on CTR and then has to be pulled after a complaint costs more than the lift was worth, and it resets the learning on the ad set.

Test the true claims against each other instead: named lender versus unnamed, "check us on RBI's list" versus no mention, KFS preview versus none. That keeps the experiment inside what the lender can defend.

> RBI's directory tells a borrower who is lending. It has never told them who is safe, and no ad should say it does.

## A practical review step

Add one line to your ad review checklist: every trust or regulatory claim must trace to a sentence in a document the lender can produce. For a regulated-entity claim that is the RBI licence or registration certificate of the bank or NBFC. For a directory claim it is the CIMS listing, described only as a listing.
