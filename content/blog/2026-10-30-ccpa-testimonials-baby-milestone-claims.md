A specific outcome in your ad needs evidence that links your product to that outcome. Parent testimonials do not count, and neither does a "results may vary" line. That is the working rule from the Central Consumer Protection Authority's final order of 25 February 2026 against Raising Superstars Enterprises, which ended in a Rs 8 lakh penalty.

The ad promised that children enrolled in its "Prodigy Framework Program" would be crawling at 3 months, walking at 8 months and using 200+ words by 18 months. The product was a set of activity videos for children aged 0 to 6. Nobody was selling medicine, and the fine landed anyway.

## What the order actually found

The case started with a referral from the Advertising Standards Council of India. The show cause notice went out on 17 May 2022, so this was not a quick action. It ran through years of hearings, with the company asked repeatedly to produce studies, expert credentials and enrolment data.

The company's defence had three parts. The claims reflected real parent experiences. Disclaimers had been added saying results are not guaranteed and vary by child. And the content had since been changed or removed.

The CCPA rejected all three. It found no "scientific study, clinical assessment, empirical research, or quantified dataset" linking the programme to the milestones it advertised, and Inc42 reports the authority saying the company "has no objective data to back its claim." On the disclaimer, the order is reported as saying that it cannot cure a fundamentally misleading claim.

The claims were treated as a misleading advertisement under Section 2(28) of the Consumer Protection Act, 2019. The company was also faulted under Rule 4 of the Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020, for lacking an adequate grievance redressal mechanism, with no customer support number on its platform. Legal commentary on the order reports a 15 day window to deposit the penalty and a compliance affidavit to follow.

## Why a testimonial cannot carry a number

A testimonial says one family had one experience. An ad that says "walking at 8 months" says the product produces that outcome for the person reading it. The gap between those two statements is the whole case.

Infant development varies widely, and the CCPA said as much. If a parent's baby walked at 8 months, there is no way to know whether the programme caused it. The ad turned an anecdote into a promise, and a promise needs data on a defined group, a method, and a sample size you are willing to show.

This is the same logic we flagged in our post on [placement numbers needing a denominator](/blog/asci-placement-claims-denominator-edtech). A number in an ad is read as typical. If it is not typical, the ad has to say so in the claim itself, not in the footer.

> A disclaimer sits next to a claim. It does not undo it.

## The three habits this order punishes

Most of the creative briefs we see in education and parenting categories carry at least one of these.

| Habit | What it looks like in an ad | Why it fails |
|---|---|---|
| Testimonial as proof | "My son was reading at 3 after the course" | One case, no method, no sample |
| Disclaimer as cover | "Results may vary" in small type under a hard number | Does not cure a misleading headline claim |
| Fix after the fact | Pulling the claim once a complaint arrives | The violation covers the period the ad ran |

The third row is the one teams underestimate. The company had changed its website by the time the order came. The penalty followed anyway, because the harm happened while the claims were live. Taking an ad down limits future exposure. It does not erase the past.

## The audience raises the bar

The authority described parents of young children as a particularly vulnerable group of consumers. Reports of the order note that the programme drew a very large user base while the ads ran, though the figures differ between sources, so we will not quote one.

For anyone running paid social to parents, this matters more than the penalty size. Rs 8 lakh is small next to the media spend behind a campaign that scales. The larger cost is a finding on record that your category's claims were unsubstantiated, plus a compliance affidavit and a regulator who now reads your next ad.

Parents are also the audience most likely to share an ad in a family group, screenshot it, and complain to ASCI. The route from a screenshot to a CCPA file is shorter than most brands assume.

## What we change in a brief now

We treat every outcome claim as one of three kinds and handle each differently.

A process claim describes what the product does: "daily 15 minute activity videos designed with early childhood educators." You can say this if it is true and you can show who the educators were.

A population claim gives a result for a group: "In our 2025 cohort of 900 families, 61% reported X." You can say this if you hold the survey, the method and the question wording, and the ad states the sample.

An individual outcome claim promises what this viewer's child will do. Unless you have controlled evidence, do not run it. A testimonial can appear as a personal story, clearly framed as one family's account, but it should not be cut into a headline or a thumbnail with a number on it.

## What to do this week

Pull every live ad and landing page that states a specific age, score, rank or time-bound result. For each one, write down the document that proves it, who produced it, and the date. If the answer is "testimonials" or "our internal feel for it", the claim is a candidate for rewording or removal.

Then check the footer. If the disclaimer is doing the work of making a headline true, rewrite the headline. And confirm your contact and grievance details are visible on every storefront or app page, since that gap was penalised in this order alongside the claims.

One honest caveat: this is a single CCPA order, and it turned on the absence of any evidence at all. A brand with a real study can still be challenged on how well it supports the exact wording. We are not lawyers, so have counsel review claims in regulated or health-adjacent categories.
