Every real estate advertisement in India, on every medium, has to carry the project's RERA registration number, a QR code linking to the project's page on the state authority's website, and the promoter's contact details. That applies to a newspaper ad, a hoarding, a Google display banner, and an Instagram Story exactly the same way. Most developer campaigns running right now do not fully meet that bar, and the gap is usually not intentional — it is a creative team optimising a layout for a thumb-stopping ratio without knowing the compliance block was never optional.

This is Section 11(2) of the Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, and it is enforced. Every advertisement or prospectus a promoter issues has to be consistent with the sanctioned plans, layouts and specifications on file with the authority, and it has to prominently carry the authority's website address. State RERAs have been treating "prominently" literally: several 2026 directions specify the registration block at the top right corner of the creative, in a minimum font size, not buried in a corner disclaimer nobody reads.

## What actually changed this year

The part worth paying attention to isn't the registration number requirement itself — that has existed since RERA came into force. It's that state authorities have started policing the *language* around it, not just its presence.

Two specific patterns are now flagged:

- **Vague catch-all disclaimers.** "T&C apply" as a substitute for an actual, verifiable claim is being treated the same as making no disclosure at all.
- **Unverifiable amenity claims.** Phrases like "100+ amenities" that don't correspond to anything listed in the sanctioned project plan are now a direct compliance flag, not just a marketing exaggeration everyone quietly accepts.

Both of these are extremely common in real estate ad copy precisely because they're persuasive shorthand. "100+ amenities" reads better in a headline than "a gym, two courts and a landscaped podium," which is exactly why regulators went after it — the vaguer a claim, the harder it is to hold anyone to it, and that was the point being closed.

## The penalty is not hypothetical

Centrik's RERA case-law tracker records a straightforward example: MahaRERA fined a promoter ₹50,000 for publishing a newspaper advertisement that simply omitted the project's MahaRERA registration number. Punjab's authority has pursued the same violation for ads run across print and visual media. In a separate case, an advertisement describing amenities — a squash court, a state-of-the-art clubhouse, a covered pool, a spa — that were not actually part of the sanctioned project was found to violate both Section 11(2) and Section 13(1).

Penalties for advertising violations under RERA range from roughly ₹10,000 at the low end to as much as ₹50,00,000 depending on severity, and beyond the fine itself, an authority can simply direct a promoter to withdraw or modify the ad. For a paid campaign that's already spent budget building an audience around a specific creative, a takedown order is the more expensive outcome — the fine is rarely the part that actually hurts.

## Why this is a media-buying problem, not just a legal one

The instinct on a performance team is to treat the RERA block the way a pharma account treats a side-effects panel: as dead weight competing with the actual creative for attention, something to shrink until it barely clears the compliance bar. That instinct is exactly backwards here, for a specific reason — this requirement follows the ad onto every platform, including the ones where nobody expects to see it.

> A creative that clears review on the developer's website and then gets cropped, resized, or auto-generated into a dozen ad formats for a Performance Max or Advantage+ campaign needs the compliance block to survive every single one of those variants, not just the original.

That is where most real breaches actually happen — not in the hero creative someone reviewed carefully, but in the sixth automatically-generated square crop that a platform's dynamic creative tool produced without anyone checking it against the sanctioned plan.

## A five-point check before the next campaign goes live

| Check | What it actually means |
| --- | --- |
| Registration number visible | Present and legible in every crop and aspect ratio the campaign will actually run, not just the master file |
| QR code present and functional | Links to the correct project page on the state authority's site — test it, don't assume it |
| No unverifiable amenity claims | Every amenity named in copy exists in the sanctioned plan on file |
| No vague disclaimers | "T&C apply" replaced with the specific, checkable claim it was standing in for |
| Consistency across auto-generated variants | Someone has actually looked at what the ad platform generated, not just the original design |

None of this is difficult work. It's boring, unglamorous checklist work, which is exactly why it gets skipped when a campaign is already three days behind its go-live date.

## What to do this week

Pull every live real estate ad set — organic and paid, across every platform — and run it against the five checks above. Do this before the next campaign push, not as a retrospective audit after something gets flagged. If your agency is generating dynamic creative variants automatically, add a step to actually review a sample of what got produced, because that's where the registration block quietly disappears.
