Most disclosure failures we audit are not word choice. The label is usually fine. It flashes for two seconds at the top of a 90 second Reel, sits at the end of a hashtag block, or lives in the bio instead of the post. ASCI's Guidelines for Influencer Advertising in Digital Media set a duration and a position for the label, and those are the two rules that decide whether a post passes.

Start with the part everyone gets wrong in the other direction.

## ASCI permits ten labels, and #Collab is one of them

The guidelines list the permitted disclosure labels explicitly: Advertisement, Ad, Sponsored, Collaboration, Partnership, Employee, Free gift, Affiliate, the "Paid Partnership" tag on Instagram, and the "Includes Paid Promotion" tag on YouTube. Any one or more of them may be used.

So the widely repeated claim that #Collab is not a valid disclosure in India is wrong. Collaboration is on the list. Partnership is on the list. A creator who writes #Collab at the top of a caption has used a permitted label.

The catch is that the platform tag is treated as an addition, not a replacement. The guidelines say a platform's disclosure tool "should be considered in addition to an influencer's own disclosure." Turning on Instagram's Paid Partnership toggle and writing nothing in the caption leaves you leaning on a label that a viewer scrolling a muted Reel may never register.

## The duration rule that fails most Reels

This is the specific bit that gets skipped in briefs. ASCI ties label duration to video length:

| Video length | How long the disclosure label must stay |
| --- | --- |
| 15 seconds or less | At least 3 seconds |
| Longer than 15 seconds, under 2 minutes | One third of the video's length |
| 2 minutes or longer | The entire duration of the section where the brand or its benefits are mentioned |

Run those numbers against a real edit. A 45 second Reel needs the label visible for 15 seconds, not a two second title card. A 9 minute YouTube integration that mentions the brand across three separate segments needs the label present through all three, not once at the start.

For live streams, the label must be announced at the beginning and at the end of the broadcast, and if the recording stays up afterwards, the disclosure has to be added to the caption. For audio, it must be announced at the start, at the end, and before and after every break. For a picture or video post with no accompanying text, an Instagram story or a Snap, the label has to be superimposed on the visual itself.

> The disclosure is not a credit line at the end. It is a condition the post has to satisfy while the selling is happening.

Placement follows the same logic. The guidelines call out disclosures that appear only on an About Me page, a profile, a bio, at the end of a post or video, or anywhere requiring a click on MORE. Those are treated as likely to be missed. A label buried in a group of hashtags or links is treated the same way.

Language matters too. The label must be in English or in the language of the advertisement itself, in a form an average consumer understands. A Marathi Reel carrying an English label the audience does not read is a defensible position on paper and a weak one in practice.

## Barter and gifting are still advertising

The material connection definition in the guidelines is broad on purpose. It covers monetary compensation, free products with or without conditions attached including unsolicited ones, discounts, gifts, contest and sweepstakes entries, trips, hotel stays, media barters, coverage, awards, and family or employment relationships.

Two consequences follow that clients rarely price in. First, disclosure is required even when the review is genuinely unbiased and entirely the creator's own words, because the trigger is the connection, not the sentiment. Second, a PR box sent with no ask attached still creates the obligation the moment the creator posts about it.

We look at this every time a client runs a seeding programme. The seeding list is usually five to ten times the size of the paid list, it is managed by a different team, and nobody has told those creators anything about labels. That is where the exposure sits.

## If you argue there was no material connection, you need paper

The addendum to the guidelines sets out what ASCI will accept if an advertiser or influencer disputes that a post was an advertisement at all. It is a declaration from the advertiser confirming there was no material connection as on the date of the post, signed by a senior member of the organisation: a marketing head, legal or compliance head, digital marketing head or equivalent.

Where the advertiser cannot reasonably be traced, or the post features brands from several advertisers, proof of purchase supplied by the influencer is accepted instead.

That is a meaningful operational detail. The defence is not an email from a brand manager. It is a signed statement from someone senior, produced after the fact, about a post nobody was tracking. Keeping a dated record of who received what is cheaper than assembling that later.

## Virtual influencers carry a second disclosure

A virtual influencer must additionally disclose that the audience is not interacting with a real human being, and that disclosure must be upfront and prominent as well. It sits alongside the paid label, not instead of it. If you are running an AI persona for a brand, the post carries two things: the ad label and the not-a-human label.

## Health and finance need a qualification, not just a label

On 28 April 2025 ASCI published an update to Addendum 2, dated 7 April 2025, covering influencers in BFSI and health and nutrition. The earlier position required qualifications from anyone advising on or commenting on the merits of goods and services in those fields. The update narrows it: qualifications must be held and declared where technical information and advice are given, and are not required where the content is generic or a public service message.

ASCI's own examples of the generic case are an insurance company using an influencer to talk about the value of annual health check-ups, and a health food company working with a chef to promote a meal service. Manisha Kapoor, ASCI's CEO and Secretary General, framed the update as bringing nuance for influencers operating in BFSI and health and nutrition.

Sitting above ASCI is the statutory layer. The Department of Consumer Affairs issued Endorsement Know-hows for celebrities, influencers and virtual influencers on 20 January 2023, and Additional Influencer Guidelines for Health and Wellness Celebrities, Influencers and Virtual Influencers on 10 August 2023. Those require certified practitioners to state their credentials, and require anyone presenting as a health expert to carry a disclaimer that the content is not a substitute for professional medical advice. Breaches fall under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, not a self-regulatory rap on the knuckles.

## What to change this week

Put duration in the brief, not in the review. If the deliverable is a 45 second Reel, write "label visible for 15 seconds minimum, burned into the frame" into the scope of work, because a creator cannot retrofit that without a re-edit and the re-edit is the thing that never happens.

Then audit the seeded list, not just the paid one. In our experience that is where the undisclosed posts are, and the advertiser carries responsibility for the content of the advertisement regardless of who pressed publish.

The honest caveat: none of this makes a claim substantiable. Disclosure and claim substantiation are separate obligations, and a perfectly labelled post making an unsupported health or returns claim is still a problem.
