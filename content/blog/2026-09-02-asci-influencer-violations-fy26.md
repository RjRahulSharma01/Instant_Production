ASCI processed 1,609 influencer advertisements in FY 2025-26 and asked for modifications to 97.3% of them. The interesting number isn't that one. It's that more than 54% of those ads were promoting categories that are prohibited by law or restricted for advertising in the first place. The disclosure tag was never the deciding factor.

That comes from the Annual Complaints Report 2025-26, which the Advertising Standards Council of India released on 28 May 2026. Most coverage led with the headline: 11,581 cases reviewed, up 21% year on year, 9,841 ads scrutinised, 98% requiring some change. The influencer section is the part worth reading twice if you brief creators for a living.

## The failure is upstream of the caption

Brands and agencies have spent three years training creators on where to put "#ad". That work was necessary and it partly landed — ASCI's half-yearly report in November 2025 put voluntary compliance among influencers at 90%, meaning creators fix things once they're caught.

But the FY 2025-26 breakdown shows the bulk of what's being caught is not a formatting error. Ads promoting harmful products or situations made up 75.4% of all violations across the year. Misleading claims came second at 27.5%. Those are decisions made in the brief and the deal memo, weeks before anyone types a caption.

Here is the split ASCI published for influencer violations by category:

| Category | Share of influencer violations |
| --- | --- |
| Illegal / offshore betting | 54% |
| Personal care | 16.9% |
| Electronics and consumer durables | 7.9% |
| Food and beverages | 6.3% |
| Fashion and lifestyle | 4.3% |

Betting dominating the list is a distinct problem — ASCI identified 854 influencer violations tied to the offshore betting ecosystem between April and December 2025, including accounts running nothing else. If you're a legitimate brand, that line isn't about you.

The next four are.

## Personal care is where honest brands get caught

Strip out betting and personal care becomes the single biggest source of influencer violations, at 16.9%. ASCI was specific about what kind of line fails: claims of visible, rapid or guaranteed results, presented as certain or universal, without scientific substantiation behind them.

The report names the shapes these take. Impossible timelines — dandruff gone in "1 wash". Manufactured precision — "11.7x stronger hair". Superlatives like "India's No.1". "Natural" and "safe" positioning doing quiet work that the formulation can't back.

Every one of those reads like something a creator would say naturally, in their own voice, on camera. That's precisely why it slips through. The brand approves a script that says "visibly reduces hair fall", the creator delivers it as "my hair fall stopped in two weeks", and the substantiation file behind the claim no longer covers what was said.

Most beauty clients we work with have a claims document. Almost none of them have shared it with the creator. That gap is the entire problem, and it costs nothing to close — a one-page list of what the product can and cannot be said to do, sent with the brief.

Food and beverage brands have a sharper version of the same issue. Nutraceuticals were the largest subcategory in F&B cases at 52%, and 96% of those required modification. ASCI flagged claims around metabolic health, chronic disease, child development, fertility and organ function on products classified as food under FSSAI rules — where therapeutic language is not available to you regardless of how the creator phrases it.

## Where the ads are, and who is looking

Digital accounted for 97.3% of all ads ASCI scrutinised in FY 2025-26. Of those, 82% were sponsored content on social platforms, and Meta platforms alone made up 79.84% of digital violations. TV and print, by contrast, showed near-perfect adherence at 97% voluntary compliance.

The other structural change: 93% of ASCI's cases in FY 2025-26 came from its own proactive monitoring, not from consumer complaints. The old assumption — that a small campaign in a niche category is unlikely to be seen — no longer holds. ASCI's surveillance now finds ads at a scale a complaint queue never could, and it shares what it finds with the Ministry of Information and Broadcasting and, for real estate, with state RERAs including Telangana's.

> The report's most damning phrase isn't a statistic. It's ASCI Chairman Sudhanshu Vats describing "the normalisation of non-compliance as a post-publication correction exercise."

That is a fair description of how a lot of influencer marketing is run right now. Ship it, and fix it if someone objects. Voluntary compliance rose from 83% to 86% and 61% of ads were withdrawn or modified without contest — numbers ASCI reads as a healthy self-regulation system, and which also happen to describe an industry that treats correction as the process rather than the exception.

## What we've changed on our side

Three things, none of them expensive.

**Category clearance before creator outreach.** Before we shortlist a single creator, someone confirms the product's category is one that can legally be advertised, and by whom. For a nutraceutical, a fintech offer, or anything adjacent to health, that check involves the client's regulatory contact, not the marketing team. This is the step that would have caught 54% of ASCI's flagged influencer ads.

**A claims sheet that travels with the brief.** Two columns: approved phrasing, and the substantiation behind it. A third column listing what must never be said — timelines, cure language, comparative superlatives. The creator gets this, not just the brand manager.

**Approval on the delivered cut, not the script.** Violations live in ad libs. A creator who was briefed correctly can still say "gone in a week" because it sounded better on camera. We review the actual file that will be posted.

None of this slows a campaign down by more than a day. Building it into how you run [influencer marketing](/services/influencer-marketing) is a lot cheaper than a takedown mid-flight, and for regulated categories like [beauty and wellness](/industries/beauty) it's the difference between a claim you can defend and one you quietly delete.

## The honest caveat

ASCI has no statutory power to fine you. It can ask for modification, publish the case, and pass the file to a regulator that does — the CCPA, the MIB, FSSAI, a state RERA. So the direct cost of an ASCI action is usually a pulled post and a wasted media budget, not a penalty.

The indirect cost is the referral. And separately from all of this, if you're paying creators in product rather than cash, there's a tax exposure that has nothing to do with ASCI at all — we covered [what barter deals actually cost you](/blog/influencer-barter-tds-gst) last week.

Start with the category check. It's one question, asked before you spend anything, and by ASCI's own numbers it's where more than half the failures begin.
