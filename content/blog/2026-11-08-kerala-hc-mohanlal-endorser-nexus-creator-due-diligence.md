The Kerala High Court has ruled that a brand ambassador is not automatically liable for a company's unfair trade practice. Endorsers answer under Section 21 of the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, and only if they have no direct link to the consumer's transaction does the wider unfair-trade-practice route close. Section 21 itself is untouched, and it is the one that can cost a creator Rs 10 lakh.

## What the court actually decided

Two customers pledged gold ornaments with Manappuram Finance. They said advertisements featuring the actor Mohanlal led them to expect a 12% interest rate, and they were charged more when they came to close the loan. They filed a complaint before the District Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission in Thiruvananthapuram, naming the company and the actor.

Justice Ziyad Rahman A.A. quashed the proceedings against Mohanlal. As reported, the court held that an endorser can be pulled into a claim of deficiency of service or unfair trade practice only where there is a direct link between the consumer and the endorser. Appearing in the ad did not create that link. Nothing showed the actor persuaded these customers to take the loan, and the rate assurance came from the finance company.

The case against Manappuram Finance continues. The ruling moves the endorser out of a transaction dispute. It does not move the brand anywhere.

## What it did not decide

The same reporting is clear that endorsers are not immune. Section 21, the provision on false or misleading advertisements, still applies to them, and the court noted that an endorser who fails to vet an advertisement with due diligence can face action under it.

That matters more for a creator than for a film star. A creator is usually the only person who shot, wrote and posted the claim. There is rarely a manager, a bank or a legal team standing between the endorser and the audience.

## The penalties still on the books

The penalty provisions below are as summarised by Argus Partners and the Press Information Bureau release on the 2022 guidelines.

| Provision | What it allows |
|---|---|
| Section 21(2) | Fine of up to Rs 10 lakh on a manufacturer or endorser of a false or misleading ad |
| Section 21(2), repeat | Up to Rs 50 lakh for a subsequent offence |
| Section 21(3) | Bar on the endorser from endorsing any product or service for up to 1 year |
| Section 21(3), repeat | Up to 3 years for a repeat endorser offence |
| Section 21(5) | No penalty under 21(2) or 21(3) if the endorser exercised due diligence to verify the claims |

The last row is the one to build a process around. Due diligence is a defence, which means the endorser has to be able to show it.

## What due diligence looks like for a creator

The Guidelines for Prevention of Misleading Advertisements and Endorsements for Misleading Advertisements, 2022, notified by the Central Consumer Protection Authority on 9 June 2022, say an endorsement must reflect the genuine and reasonably current opinion of the endorser. It must rest on adequate information about, or experience with, the product or service.

In practice that gives you two tests for every post. Has the creator actually used or experienced the product? And has someone checked that the brand can substantiate the specific claim being made?

> A signature on the brief is not due diligence. A file showing who checked the claim, against what, and when, is.

The second test is where campaigns fail. A creator who repeats "results in 7 days" has no way to verify it. The brand has to hand over the test report, the label approval or the licence before the shoot, not after a notice arrives.

## How we set this up with brands

Most clients we work with already collect the creative approval from legal. What is usually missing is the reverse flow, from brand to creator. We ask for four things before a creator posts.

- The claim list: every factual statement the creator is allowed to make, in the brand's words.
- The substantiation for each claim, or a line saying it is a subjective opinion.
- Confirmation that the creator has used the product, with the date they started.
- A written note that the creator may not add claims beyond the list.

## Where brands should not read this ruling

The finance company in this case is still a respondent. A brand cannot point to the Kerala order and conclude that it carries less risk when a creator speaks for it. If anything, the order makes the brand the first stop for a consumer complaint, because the endorser is harder to reach in a transaction dispute.

It is also a single High Court order on a dispute over a loan, and the reports available do not give the judgment date or a formal case caption. It does not bind the CCPA's own enforcement under Section 21, and it says nothing about ASCI's complaint process, which runs separately and does not depend on a purchase.

Pull your three most recent creator briefs. For each, check that there is a claim list, a substantiation file for any factual claim, and a record that the creator used the product.
