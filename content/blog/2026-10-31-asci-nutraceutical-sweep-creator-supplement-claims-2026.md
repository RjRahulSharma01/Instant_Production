ASCI reviewed 61 nutraceutical ads between July and September 2026 and every one of the 60 closed cases needed changes. Thirty-one advertisers contested the objection and none could back the claim. If a creator reads your supplement brief out loud, that proof gap becomes theirs too.

The report is called "A Pill for Thought", and it was published at the end of October. It matters for anyone briefing creators because ASCI went looking for these ads itself. Nobody complained first.

The practical point for a creator brief is simple. A creator is not a lab, and nobody expects them to verify a formulation. They can only repeat what the brand hands over, so the brand has to hand over claims it can defend.

## What ASCI actually did

This was a suo-motu sweep, meaning ASCI searched for the ads on its own rather than waiting for a consumer complaint. The outcome split almost evenly: 29 advertisers modified or withdrew the ad without a fight, and 31 contested it and lost. One case is still being processed, which is why you will see both "60" and "61" in coverage.

The brands were mostly D2C. MediaNama's report names Wellbeing Nutrition, Fast&Up, Chicnutrix, Veera Health and Muditam among 32 brands, and says six advertisers accounted for 24 of the ads. The report does not say where the ads ran, so do not assume it was a Meta or YouTube problem. It is a claims problem that travels with the script.

## The three failure patterns

ASCI described three patterns: claims without adequate proof, superiority claims with no clear benchmark, and promises made ahead of proof. In practice that looks like this:

| What the ad said | Why it failed |
|---|---|
| "Lose 6 kgs in 60 days" | Timeline promise with no product-level evidence |
| "Your skin, but 7 years younger" | Anti-ageing outcome from a supplement, unproven |
| "Three times faster absorption" | Superiority claim with no stated comparator |
| Black-to-pink lung imagery for smokers | Detox and organ-cleansing claim |
| "Reverse PCOD" | Disease claim, which food rules do not allow |

Two details stand out. Some products used names resembling GLP-1 drugs, and five ads pitched berberine as a safer alternative to prescription therapy for weight and Type 2 diabetes. ASCI also flagged glutathione ads that showed darker skin turning lighter.

## The ingredient study is not your proof

The most useful line in the report for agencies comes from ASCI's Manisha Kapoor, who said research on an individual ingredient cannot automatically substantiate the efficacy of a finished product. Most brands in the sweep leaned on ingredient-level studies. ASCI wants evidence for your formulation, at your dose.

> Substantiation should precede the promise, not follow a complaint.

That sentence from the report is the whole compliance model in one line, and it is the reverse of how most creator briefs are written. Briefs usually go out first and the "claims document" gets assembled when someone objects.

## Why this lands on creators and agencies

ASCI is a self-regulatory body, not a statutory regulator. It cannot fine anyone, and courts have held its recommendations are not binding on non-members. That is the comfortable reading, and it is incomplete.

ASCI reports non-complying advertisers to FSSAI under a 2016 MoU, and FSSAI rules bar food products from claiming to prevent, treat or cure disease. ASCI and the CCPA also announced an agreement in March 2024. Under Section 21 of the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, the CCPA can fine manufacturers and endorsers up to Rs 10 lakh, rising to Rs 50 lakh for repeat violations, and can bar an endorser for up to one year, or three years for repeat violations.

MediaNama notes the report does not say whether any of these 60 cases went to FSSAI or the CCPA. Treat that as an open question, not as comfort. For the wider picture on how endorser liability works, our post on [FSSAI wellness claim copy](/blog/fssai-wellness-claim-copy-2026) covers the food-side rules, and the [creator credentials piece](/blog/asci-health-finance-creator-credentials-line) covers who can say what.

## What we change in a supplement brief

Most clients we work with in wellness have a claims document somewhere. The sweep shows the problem is usually that it is not attached to the brief. Here is what we now do before a creator shoots:

1. Split every claim into three buckets: permitted structure/function wording, claims needing product-level evidence, and claims we refuse outright (disease names, "reverse", "cure", organ detox).
2. Ask for the study on the finished product and the dose. If the only support is a paper on the ingredient, the claim moves to the refuse bucket or gets softened to what the label itself says.
3. Remove every timeline ("in 60 days", "in 12 weeks") unless a trial on that product shows it.
4. Drop comparative words such as "faster", "better absorbed" and "first" unless the brief names the comparator and the evidence.
5. Check product names against prescription drugs. If a name echoes a drug class, rename it before the creative is made.
6. Give creators a short list of approved lines and tell them to stay inside it, including in stories and replies.

If you run [influencer campaigns](/services/influencer-marketing) for a supplement brand, build the claim list before you build the shortlist. Creators who ad-lib "this fixed my PCOD" on camera have just written a disease claim for you.

## An honest caveat

None of this guarantees a clean result. ASCI said every ad it assessed needed some modification, so the bar is high and the bar keeps moving. What you can control is whether the proof exists before the post goes live. If it does not, the cheapest edit is the claim, not the creator.

Next step: pull the last three supplement briefs you sent out, and for each claim write the name of the study that backs the finished product. Any claim with a blank next to it comes out.
